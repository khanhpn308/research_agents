## 1. Why this paper matters to my current research direction

P1 asks whether a simplified continuum-like representation can replace an explicit high-layer-count model while retaining the mechanics needed to predict when it works and when it fails. Zhang et al. is critical because it does not merely mention continuum modeling: it constructs a “continuum-based layer jamming model” (CLJM), derives stress fields and slip-state boundaries, compares those fields with finite-element analysis (FEA), and compares predicted cantilever load–deflection response with an experiment. **VERIFIED FULL TEXT — Zhang 2025, Abstract and Sects. 2–6, printed pp. 821–829 / PDF pp. 1–9.**

The progression of the verified literature is:

1. **Narang 2018:** established the three mechanical regimes—pre-slip, transition/progressive slip, and full slip—and developed a rigorous analytical solution for a two-layer cantilever. Many-layer behavior was handled with explicit frictional-contact FEA and experiments. Narang also noted that FEA execution time increased linearly with layer count and proposed, as future limiting behavior, approximating a fixed-thickness, very-high-layer-count stack as a single crystal with a single slip system. That proposal was not itself the validated continuum model later supplied by Zhang. **VERIFIED FULL TEXT — Narang 2018, Sects. 2.1–2.2 and 3.3, PDF pp. 3–4 and 7.**
2. **Caruso 2023:** supplied an analytical, explicitly layer/interface-aware baseline for an arbitrary even number of layers in three-point bending. It predicts discrete, sequential slip events from the central interface outward, piecewise stiffness reduction, pressure and friction effects, hysteresis, and overhang-induced stiffness. It was tested at 8, 12, 16, and 20 layers and at 24, 48, and 68 kPa. **VERIFIED FULL TEXT — Caruso 2023, Sects. 2–5, PDF pp. 3–8.**
3. **Zhang 2025:** replaces the layer/interface index by continuous through-height stress fields and a moving boundary between a central sliding region and outer jammed regions. It aims to avoid layer-by-layer bookkeeping while predicting stress and deformation in full-jamming, half-slipping, and full-slipping states. **VERIFIED FULL TEXT — Zhang 2025, Sects. 2–4, printed pp. 822–826 / PDF pp. 2–6.**

Therefore Zhang directly attacks the representational premise behind P1. What remains to be tested is not whether any continuum model exists—it does—but whether its validity and breakdown boundaries have already been mapped systematically across layer count, pressure, curvature/load, and slip regime. This last sentence is a framing question, not a novelty claim.

## 2. Physical system before mathematics

### 2.1 What the beam is

Imagine many thin, flat sheets stacked through a total height (h), each sheet having thickness \(\delta\), width \(b\), and length \(L\). The stack is enclosed in a flexible airtight membrane. Removing air produces a pressure difference (p) across the envelope. That pressure presses the sheets together. The pressure itself does not glue them; it creates normal contact, which enables tangential friction.

The simplest friction capacity used by Zhang is Coulomb friction:

\[
|\tau| \le \mu p,
\]

where \(\tau\) is interlayer shear stress, \(\mu\) is the dimensionless friction coefficient, and \(p\) is pressure in pascals. Thus \(\mu p\) has units of pascals and is the assumed limiting shear traction. Zhang explicitly neglects any extra pressure generated by elastic deformation, so the cap is based on the imposed vacuum pressure rather than a spatially varying contact solution. **VERIFIED FULL TEXT — Zhang 2025, Sect. 2, printed p. 823 / PDF p. 3.**

### 2.2 What bending tries to make the layers do

When a beam bends, material on one side of its neutral surface wants to extend while material on the other side wants to shorten. If all interfaces stick, the layers must share a common bending kinematics and the stack behaves much like one thick beam. A thick bonded rectangle has a much larger second moment of area than the sum of the moments of its separated sheets, so the jammed stack is stiff.

The axial forces required to keep the sheets moving together create shear transfer between neighboring material levels. In Zhang's continuous idealization, this transfer is represented by a shear-stress field \(\tau(y,s)\), where \(s\) is distance along the beam's middle surface and \(y\) is position through its height.

### 2.3 The slip regimes

- **Pre-slip / full jamming:** everywhere satisfies \(|\tau|<\mu p\). The stack deforms elastically and reversibly in Zhang's idealization. Its effective bending section is the full height.
- **Partial or progressive slip / Zhang's “half-slipping” state:** the shear demand first reaches \(\mu p\) near the cross-section center, where the rectangular-beam shear profile is largest. A central sliding zone \(|y|\le y_s\) develops, while outer bands remain jammed. As load increases, the boundary \(y_s\) moves outward.
- **Full slipping:** Zhang defines the limiting state by \(y_s=h/2-\delta\). The central sliding region then occupies all but the outermost sheet-scale bands. “Full slipping” therefore does not mean that literally every material point has left the elastic region; the free outer surfaces must still carry zero shear traction, and thin outer jammed bands remain in the model. **VERIFIED FULL TEXT — Zhang 2025, Fig. 3 and Sect. 3.2, printed p. 824 / PDF p. 4.**

Physically, increasing pressure or friction raises the shear traction that can be transmitted before sliding. Increasing bending load raises internal shear and moment, pushes the sliding boundary outward, reduces the part of the section that acts as a coherently jammed bending region, and changes the load–deflection relation. Zhang represents irreversible sliding as ideal plastic-like shear deformation; it does not track each interface's relative displacement.

## 3. Why a discrete model becomes difficult

### A. Explicit discrete-layer/interface representation

For \(N\) layers there are \(N-1\) physical interfaces. A discrete model can assign separate displacement fields to every layer and separate stick/slip conditions to every interface. In Caruso's analytical model, slip begins at the central interface and propagates outward through a sequence of symmetric interface events. For even \(N\), the published general expressions explicitly enumerate critical steps up to an index of order \(N/2\). **VERIFIED FULL TEXT — Caruso 2023, Sect. 2.2, Eqs. (8)–(15), PDF p. 4.**

In a discrete FEA model, each layer also requires its own mesh and contact pair(s). Narang states that many-layer FEA execution time scaled linearly with layer count; Caruso likewise states that computational time increases with layer count and may become prohibitive. **VERIFIED FULL TEXT — Narang 2018, Sect. 3.3, PDF p. 7; Caruso 2023, Introduction, PDF p. 2.**

Exactly what grows with \(N\) is therefore:

- the number of layer bodies or layer kinematic fields: \(N\);
- the number of interfaces: \(N-1\);
- the number of possible discrete slip transitions in a symmetric analytical description: order \(N/2\);
- the contact constraints and layer meshes in explicit FEA;
- the algebra required to join all stick/slip regions and critical loads.

The first three counts follow directly from geometry and Caruso's indexed model; describing the resulting implementation burden as growth in computational state is **INFERENCE**. Only Narang's linear FEA timing statement is a reported scaling measurement.

### B. Continuum representation

Zhang lets \(\delta/h\to0\) while total height \(h\) stays fixed, equivalently \(N=h/\delta\to\infty\). The layer number is replaced by continuous dependence on \(y\). Instead of asking whether interface 7 or 8 has slipped, the model asks where the continuous slip boundary \(y_s(s)\) lies. This replaces a growing set of interface states with fields \(\sigma(y,s)\), \(\tau(y,s)\), and a moving boundary. **VERIFIED FULL TEXT — Zhang 2025, Sect. 2, printed pp. 822–823 / PDF pp. 2–3.**

The analytical equation count then does not grow by adding one equation for each interface. That layer-count independence is **INFERENCE from the published equation architecture**, not a timing benchmark: Zhang reports no wall-clock comparison against Caruso or discrete FEA.

## 4. What Zhang's continuum model actually is

### 4.1 What becomes continuous

Zhang treats the following as continuous functions:

- longitudinal normal stress \(\sigma(y,s)\) [Pa];
- interlayer-like shear stress \(\tau(y,s)\) [Pa];
- longitudinal normal strain \(\varepsilon_s(y,s)\) [dimensionless];
- shear strain \(\gamma_{sy}(y,s)\) [dimensionless];
- beam rotation \(\theta(s)\) [rad];
- curvature \(1/r=d\theta/ds\) [m\(^{-1}\)];
- the slip-front coordinate \(y_s(s)\) [m].

The discrete interface number and individual interface slip displacement are removed. The single-layer thickness \(\delta\) is not removed completely: it remains in the definition of the full-slipping limit and in \(Q_{\max}\).

### 4.2 Model architecture

The architecture is:

1. Replace the layered stack by a rectangular curved beam with continuous through-height fields.
2. Use curved-beam force and moment equilibrium to obtain section resultants \(N(s)\), \(Q(s)\), and \(M(s)\).
3. In fully jammed regions, start from the parabolic rectangular-beam shear distribution.
4. Apply the yield cap \(|\tau|=\mu p\). The capped central zone is the sliding region; solve for its boundary \(y_s\).
5. Construct piecewise shear and normal stress fields in the sliding and jammed regions.
6. Compute the inertia of the surviving jammed part and integrate moment–curvature to update rotation.
7. For large overall motion, apply the load incrementally so each step uses a small-deformation update.

This is more than an equivalent-stiffness formula because it predicts spatial stress fields and a moving slip region. It is less than an explicit discrete slip model because it has no interface-by-interface state or relative-displacement field.

### 4.3 What label is justified?

**VERIFIED FULL TEXT:** the authors call it a “continuum-based” model and derive it from the formal limit \(\delta\to0\), \(N\to\infty\), with fixed \(h\). They also describe the resulting medium as elastoplastic.

**INFERENCE:** “continuum-limit idealization” or “continuum elastoplastic surrogate” is the most precise description. The paper does not show a formal homogenization procedure with a representative volume element, scale-separated asymptotic expansion, or averaged effective tensor. Calling it “homogenized” without qualification would inflate the terminology.

## 5. Derivation of the model

### 5.1 Notation and sign convention

The paper uses \(s\) [m] along the middle surface, \(y\) [m] through height, \(L\) [m] for length, \(h\) [m] for total height, \(b\) [m] for out-of-plane width, \(\delta\) [m] for one-layer thickness, \(A=bh\) [m\(^2\)] for full cross-sectional area, \(\theta\) [rad] for section rotation, and \(r=ds/d\theta\) [m] for radius of curvature. A prime denotes differentiation with respect to \(s\). Forces \(N\) and \(Q\) are in newtons, bending moment \(M\) is in N·m, distributed loads \(q_s,q_y\) are in N/m, stresses \(\sigma,\tau,p\) are in Pa, Young's modulus \(E\) is in Pa, and \(\mu\) is dimensionless. The signs follow the arrows in the paper's Fig. 2.

### 5.2 Local friction/yield rule (unnumbered in the paper)

The paper writes the loading stages as

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{loading: }&\tau<\mu p,\quad \tau\,d\tau>0,\\
\text{yielding: }&\tau=\mu p,\quad \tau\,d\tau\ge0,\\
\text{unloading: }&\tau\le\mu p,\quad \tau\,d\tau<0.
\end{aligned}
\]

Here \(\tau\) [Pa] is shear stress, \(d\tau\) [Pa] is its increment, \(p\) [Pa] is imposed vacuum pressure, and \(\mu\) [–] is friction coefficient. Elsewhere the paper uses the sign-symmetric condition \(|\tau|\) and \(\operatorname{sign}(Q)\mu p\); the unnumbered display is written for one signed loading direction. Below yield, deformation is called elastic/reversible. At yield, shear strain may change without a unique change in shear stress, which is an ideal-plastic analogy and motivates incremental solution. **VERIFIED FULL TEXT — Sect. 2, printed p. 823 / PDF p. 3.**

### 5.3 Section equilibrium: Eqs. (1)–(2)

**Equation (1):**

\[
\frac{dN}{ds}=-Q\theta'-q_s=-\frac{Q}{r}-q_s,\qquad
\frac{dQ}{ds}=N\theta'+q_y=\frac{N}{r}+q_y,\qquad
\frac{dM}{ds}=Q.
\]

Every symbol is as defined in Sect. 5.1: \(N,Q\) [N] are axial and transverse section forces, \(M\) [N·m] is bending moment, \(q_s,q_y\) [N/m] are distributed-load components, \(s\) [m] is arc coordinate, \(\theta'=d\theta/ds=1/r\) [m\(^{-1}\)], and \(r\) [m] is curvature radius. This is force and moment balance for an infinitesimal curved segment. It neglects second-order infinitesimals and uses \(\cos d\theta\approx1\), \(\sin d\theta\approx d\theta\). It supplies the resultants needed by the stress calculation. **VERIFIED FULL TEXT — Zhang 2025, Sect. 3.1, printed p. 823, Eq. (1) / PDF p. 3.**

**Equation (2):** the first two balances are integrated using a rotation matrix:

\[
\begin{bmatrix}N\\Q\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{bmatrix}
\left(
\begin{bmatrix}N_0\\Q_0\end{bmatrix}
+\int_{s_0}^{s}
\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&\sin\theta\\-\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}-q_s(\xi)\\q_y(\xi)\end{bmatrix}d\xi
\right).
\]

Here \(N_0,Q_0\) [N] are internal forces at reference coordinate \(s_0\) [m], and \(\xi\) [m] is a dummy integration coordinate; \(\theta\) [rad] and the load components have the meanings above. The matrices rotate between local curved-beam axes and a reference frame. Reactions provide \(N_0,Q_0\), then Eq. (1)'s last relation gives \(M\) by integrating \(Q\). The printed equation suppresses some explicit \(s\)- or \(\xi\)-arguments, so this tutorial does not add unprinted dependencies. **VERIFIED FULL TEXT — Sect. 3.1, printed p. 823, Eq. (2) / PDF p. 3.**

### 5.4 Shear stress and slip-front equations: Eqs. (3)–(9)

**Equation (3), fully jammed shear field:**

\[
\tau(y,Q)=\frac{3}{2}\frac{Q}{A}\left(1-\frac{4y^2}{h^2}\right),\qquad A=bh.
\]

Here \(\tau\) [Pa] is shear stress, \(Q\) [N] is section shear force, \(y,h,b\) [m] locate and size the rectangle, and \(A\) [m\(^2\)] is area. It is the classical parabolic shear profile for a rectangular straight beam, borrowed because Zhang assumes \(r\gg h\). Its maximum is at \(y=0\), which explains why yielding begins at the center. **VERIFIED FULL TEXT — Sect. 3.2, printed p. 824, Eq. (3) / PDF p. 4.**

**Equation (4), equivalent shear force for the jammed bands:**

\[
Q_{\mathrm{eq}}=\operatorname{sign}(Q)\,\frac{2}{3}\,
\frac{\mu p A}{1-4y_s^2/h^2}.
\]

Here \(Q_{\mathrm{eq}}\) [N] is an auxiliary force placed into Eq. (3), \(\operatorname{sign}(Q)\) [–] supplies loading direction, \(\mu\) [–] is friction coefficient, \(p\) [Pa] is imposed pressure, \(A\) [m\(^2\)] is area, and \(y_s,h\) [m] are the sliding-boundary coordinate and height. It enforces \(\tau(y_s,Q_{\mathrm{eq}})=\operatorname{sign}(Q)\mu p\), making the outer parabolic jammed profile meet the friction cap continuously. **VERIFIED FULL TEXT — printed p. 824, Eq. (4) / PDF p. 4.**

**Equations (5) and (6), state thresholds:**

\[
Q_{\mathrm{slip}}=\frac{2}{3}\mu pbh,
\tag{5}
\]

\[
Q_{\max}=\mu pb\,\frac{3h^2-6\delta h+4\delta^2}{3(h-\delta)}.
\tag{6}
\]

Both \(Q_{\mathrm{slip}}\) and \(Q_{\max}\) are forces [N]. The remaining symbols are \(\mu\) [–], \(p\) [Pa], and \(b,h,\delta\) [m]. Equation (5) is obtained by setting the maximum of Eq. (3), at \(y=0\), equal to \(\mu p\); it marks full-jamming to half-slipping. Equation (6) sets \(y_s=h/2-\delta\), integrates shear over the section, and marks the model's full-slipping limit. Both thresholds grow linearly with \(p\), \(\mu\), and \(b\). Equation (6) proves that the discrete thickness \(\delta\) survives the continuum model. **VERIFIED FULL TEXT — printed p. 824, Eqs. (5)–(6) / PDF p. 4.**

**Equation (7), complete piecewise shear field as printed:**

\[
\tau(y,s)=
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{3}{2}\dfrac{Q}{A}\left(1-\dfrac{4y^2}{h^2}\right), & |Q|\le Q_{\mathrm{slip}},\\[4pt]
\operatorname{sign}(Q)\mu p, & |y|\le y_s,\ Q_{\mathrm{slip}}\le|Q|\le Q_{\max},\\[4pt]
\operatorname{sign}(Q)\mu p\dfrac{1-4y^2/h^2}{1-4y_s^2/h^2}, & |y|>y_s,\ Q_{\mathrm{slip}}\le|Q|\le Q_{\max},\\[4pt]
c_2y^2\pm c_1y+c_0, & |y|>y_s,\ |Q|>Q_{\max}.
\end{cases}
\]

Here \(\tau\) [Pa] depends on \(y,s\) [m]; \(Q,Q_{\mathrm{slip}},Q_{\max}\) [N], \(A\) [m\(^2\)], \(h,y_s\) [m], \(\mu\) [–], and \(p\) [Pa] were defined above. The polynomial coefficients have units \(c_2\) [Pa/m\(^2\)], \(c_1\) [Pa/m], and \(c_0\) [Pa]. The first branch is elastic; the next two split the section into a capped sliding core and parabolic jammed bands; the last fits the outer bands after saturation of \(y_s\). The paper does not explicitly define the \(\pm\) choice in the last line, and the displayed cases do not explicitly restate the \(|y|\le y_s\) capped branch for \(|Q|>Q_{\max}\). The surrounding prose implies that the sliding core remains at \(\operatorname{sign}(Q)\mu p\), but that completion is **INFERENCE**, not an additional printed branch. **VERIFIED FULL TEXT with source ambiguity — printed p. 824, Eq. (7) / PDF p. 4.**

**Equation (8), full-slip outer-band coefficients:**

\[
\begin{aligned}
c_2&=-\frac{12(\mu pbh+2\mu pby_s-2Q)}{b(h-2y_s)^3},\\
c_1&=\frac{4(3Qh+6Qy_s-2\mu pbh^2-4\mu pbhy_s-8\mu pby_s^2)}{b(h-2y_s)^3},\\
c_0&=\frac{h(\mu pbh^2+2\mu pbhy_s+16\mu pby_s^2-12Qy_s)}{b(h-2y_s)^3}.
\end{aligned}
\]

All symbols were defined above; \(c_2,c_1,c_0\) carry the units stated under Eq. (7). These coefficients enforce zero shear at the free surface \(\tau(h/2,s)=0\), the yield value at \(y_s\), and the resultant condition \(\int_{-h/2}^{h/2}\tau b\,dy=Q\). Thus Eq. (8) closes the last branch of Eq. (7). It inherits the rectangular-section, fixed Coulomb cap, and prescribed \(y_s\) assumptions. **VERIFIED FULL TEXT — printed p. 824, Eq. (8) / PDF p. 4.**

**Equation (9), sliding-zone half-width:**

\[
y_s=
\begin{cases}
0, & |Q|<Q_{\mathrm{slip}},\\[3pt]
\dfrac{3Q-2\mu pA+\sqrt{3(3Q-2\mu pA)(Q+2\mu pA)}}{8\mu pb},
& Q_{\mathrm{slip}}\le Q<Q_{\max},\\[7pt]
\dfrac{-3Q+2\mu pA+\sqrt{3(3Q+2\mu pA)(Q-2\mu pA)}}{8\mu pb},
& -Q_{\max}<Q\le-Q_{\mathrm{slip}},\\[7pt]
h/2-\delta, & |Q|\ge Q_{\max}.
\end{cases}
\]

Here \(y_s,h,\delta\) are lengths [m]; \(Q,Q_{\mathrm{slip}},Q_{\max}\) are forces [N]; \(\mu\) is dimensionless; \(p\) is Pa; \(A=bh\) is m\(^2\); and \(b\) is m. Equation (9) solves the section-resultant condition using Eq. (7). It converts load directly into an internal state variable: zero sliding-zone width, a growing positive width, or saturation one layer thickness below each free surface. The positive and negative branches enforce directional symmetry. **VERIFIED FULL TEXT — printed p. 824, Eq. (9) / PDF p. 4.**

### 5.5 Normal stress: Eqs. (10)–(18)

**Equation (10), piecewise axial normal stress:**

\[
\sigma(y,s)=
\begin{cases}
N_J/A_J+K_J(y\mp y_s), & |y|>y_s,\\
\sigma_S(y), & |y|<y_s.
\end{cases}
\]

Here \(\sigma\) [Pa] is longitudinal normal stress; subscript \(J\) means jammed and \(S\) sliding; \(N_J\) [N] is axial force carried by the jammed bands; \(A_J=b(h-2y_s)\) [m\(^2\)] is their area; \(K_J\) [Pa/m] is the slope of their linear bending-stress distribution; and \(y,y_s\) [m] are height coordinates. The minus sign applies above \(+y_s\) and the plus sign below \(-y_s\). \(\sigma_S\) [Pa] is still unknown. The equation converts the discrete saw-tooth layer stress pattern into a continuous limit: linear outer branches plus an inner sliding-region field. **VERIFIED FULL TEXT — Sect. 3.2 and Fig. 4, printed p. 825, Eq. (10) / PDF p. 5.**

**Equation (11), force and moment resultants:**

\[
N=N_J+\int_{-y_s}^{y_s}\sigma_S b\,dy,
\qquad
M=M_J+\int_{-y_s}^{y_s}\sigma_S yb\,dy.
\]

Here \(N,N_J\) [N] are total and jammed-region axial forces; \(M,M_J\) [N·m] are total and jammed-region moments; \(\sigma_S\) [Pa] is sliding-region normal stress; and \(b,y,y_s\) [m] define the integration. These are definitions obtained by integrating stress over the jammed and sliding areas. They connect the local field in Eq. (10) to global section equilibrium. **VERIFIED FULL TEXT — printed p. 825, Eq. (11) / PDF p. 5.**

**Equation (12), jammed-region moment:**

\[
M_J=\frac{b}{12}(h+y_s)(h-2y_s)^2K_J.
\]

Here \(M_J\) [N·m], \(b,h,y_s\) [m], and \(K_J\) [Pa/m] have the definitions above. Integrating the linear jammed-band stress in Eq. (10) produces this relation. It allows \(K_J\) to be recovered from moment once the sliding boundary is known. **VERIFIED FULL TEXT — printed p. 825, Eq. (12) / PDF p. 5.**

**Equation (13), local stress equilibrium in the sliding region:**

\[
\frac{\partial\sigma_S}{\partial s}+\frac{\partial\tau_S}{\partial y}=0.
\]

Here \(\sigma_S\) [Pa] is longitudinal normal stress and \(\tau_S=\operatorname{sign}(Q)\mu p\) [Pa] is constant shear stress in the sliding region; \(s,y\) [m] are coordinates. Since \(\partial\tau_S/\partial y=0\), the equation gives \(\partial\sigma_S/\partial s=0\), so \(\sigma_S\) is constant along \(s\) at fixed \(y\), i.e. it may be written \(f(y)\). The paper prints \(\sigma_S=f(y)\) and then says it “depends only on \(s\),” which contradicts both the equation and the next sentence (“same value at all points with the same \(y\)”). This tutorial follows the equation and flags the prose phrase as an apparent source typo. **VERIFIED FULL TEXT with internal inconsistency — printed p. 825, Eq. (13) / PDF p. 5.**

**Equation (14), changing a height integral into path integrals:** for the geometry of Fig. 5b, the paper writes, compactly,

\[
\int_{-y_s}^{y_s}\sigma_S b\,dy
=\int_{s_0}^{s}[\sigma_S(y_s)+\sigma_S(-y_s)]b\,y_s'\,ds,
\]

\[
\int_{-y_s}^{y_s}\sigma_S yb\,dy
=\int_{s_0}^{s}[\sigma_S(y_s)-\sigma_S(-y_s)]y_s b\,y_s'\,ds.
\]

Here \(s_0,s\) [m] bound a half-slipping segment, \(y_s'=dy_s/ds\) [–], \(\sigma_S(\pm y_s)\) [Pa], \(b,y_s\) [m], and the two left-hand integrals are respectively force [N] and moment [N·m]. The full printed Eq. (14) first splits each integral into upper- and lower-boundary paths and then combines them; the force uses the sum of the two boundary stresses, whereas the moment uses their difference because the lower boundary lies at \(-y_s\). The compact form above preserves those final equalities. The conversion relies on \(d y_s=y_s' ds\), the geometry in Fig. 5, and continuity at the moving boundary. It prepares Eq. (11) for differentiation along \(s\). **VERIFIED FULL TEXT — printed p. 825, Eq. (14) / PDF p. 5.**

**Equation (15), reduced resultants after continuity:**

\[
N=N_J+\int_{s_0}^{s}\frac{2N_J}{A_J}b\,y_s'\,d\xi,
\qquad M=M_J.
\]

Here \(N,N_J\) [N], \(M,M_J\) [N·m], \(A_J\) [m\(^2\)], \(b\) [m], \(y_s'\) [–], and \(s_0,s,\xi\) [m] have been defined. Continuity gives \(\sigma_S(\pm y_s)=\sigma_J(\pm y_s)\); the symmetric boundary values eliminate the sliding-region moment contribution in this construction, yielding \(M=M_J\). This is why deformation can later be computed from the jammed region. **VERIFIED FULL TEXT — printed p. 825, Eq. (15) / PDF p. 5.**

**Equation (16), differential equation for jammed axial force:**

\[
N'=N_J'+N_J\frac{y_s'}{h/2-y_s}=-Q\theta'.
\]

Here primes mean \(d/ds\); \(N,N_J,Q\) are forces [N]; \(y_s,h\) are lengths [m]; \(y_s'\) is dimensionless; and \(\theta'\) is m\(^{-1}\). Differentiating Eq. (15) gives the middle expression; the final equality uses the axial balance from Eq. (1) with no distributed axial load \(q_s\). It is solved next for \(N_J(s)\). **VERIFIED FULL TEXT — printed p. 825, Eq. (16) / PDF p. 5.**

**Equation (17), solution for \(N_J\):**

\[
N_J=\left(\frac{2y_s}{h}-1\right)
\left[
\int_{s_0}^{s}\frac{-Q\theta'}{2y_s/h-1}\,d\xi
+\frac{N(s_0)}{2y_s(s_0)/h-1}
\right].
\]

Here all forces, coordinates, height, rotation gradient, and the dummy coordinate \(\xi\) have the units defined above. The preceding sentence identifies \(s_0\) as the boundary where \(N_J=N\); the lower-limit glyph in the PDF equation is visually unclear, so \(s_0\) is used here based on that explicit prose. This integral solves Eq. (16) and supplies \(N_J\) for Eq. (10). **VERIFIED FULL TEXT with transcription note — printed p. 826, Eq. (17) / PDF p. 6.**

**Equation (18), boundary values for the second sliding-region geometry:**

\[
\begin{aligned}
\sigma_J(s_0)&=\frac{N(s_0)}{A}+K_J(s_0)(y\mp y_s)\\
&=\frac{N(s_0)}{A}+\frac{12M(s_0)}{b(h+y_s)(h-2y_s)^2}(y\mp y_s),\\
\sigma_S(s_0)&=\frac{N(s_0)}{A}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Here \(\sigma_J,\sigma_S\) [Pa], \(N(s_0)\) [N], \(A\) [m\(^2\)], \(K_J\) [Pa/m], \(M(s_0)\) [N·m], and \(b,h,y,y_s\) [m] have their prior meanings. The equation assumes axial force is uniformly distributed at \(s_0\), then superposes the jammed-region bending gradient. It initializes the second case in Fig. 5c. The following prose prints \(N_J(s_0)=N(s_0)2y_s(s_0)/h\). That expression conflicts with the stated jammed area \(A_J=b(h-2y_s)\): uniform axial stress would imply \(N_J=N(h-2y_s)/h\). This tutorial does not silently correct the paper; the printed follow-on expression is marked **UNRESOLVED / apparent internal inconsistency**. **VERIFIED FULL TEXT — printed p. 826, Eq. (18) and following sentence / PDF p. 6.**

### 5.6 Deformation: Eqs. (19)–(20) and the incremental algorithm

**Equation (19), moment–rotation relation:**

\[
\Delta\theta(s)=\int_0^s\frac{M}{EI_J}\,d\xi.
\]

Here \(\Delta\theta\) [rad] is the rotation increment, \(s,\xi\) [m] are coordinates, \(M\) [N·m] is bending moment, \(E\) [Pa] is layer Young's modulus, and \(I_J\) [m\(^4\)] is second moment of area of the jammed region. It is the Euler–Bernoulli relation \(d\theta/ds=M/(EI)\). Equation (15) supplies \(M=M_J\), while Eq. (20) supplies the changing jammed inertia. Assumptions are Euler–Bernoulli kinematics, elastic jammed bands, and small deformation within the increment. **VERIFIED FULL TEXT — Sect. 4, printed p. 826, Eq. (19) / PDF p. 6.**

**Equation (20), jammed-region inertia, as printed:**

\[
I_J=
\begin{cases}
bh^3/12, & y_s=0,\\[3pt]
\dfrac{2}{3}\left[(h/2)^3-y_s^3\right], & y_s\ne0.
\end{cases}
\]

Here \(I_J\) should have units m\(^4\), while \(b,h,y_s\) are lengths [m]. The first branch is dimensionally correct. The second branch as printed has units m\(^3\), apparently omitting \(b\). Direct integration over the two rectangular jammed bands gives

\[
I_J^{\text{dimensionally consistent}}=\frac{2b}{3}\left[(h/2)^3-y_s^3\right],
\]

but this correction is **INFERENCE**, not the printed equation. This matters for implementation and should be checked against authors' code or clarification before reproducing the model. **VERIFIED FULL TEXT with dimensional inconsistency — printed p. 826, Eq. (20) / PDF p. 6.**

The unnumbered algorithm scales the target load by \(q^k(s)=\alpha^kq(s)\), computes \(N^k,Q^k,M^k\) from Eq. (2), computes \(y_s^k\) from Eq. (9), checks \(\Delta y_s^k=y_s^k-y_s^{k-1}\), computes a moment increment \(\Delta M^k=M^{k+1}-M^k\), obtains \(\Delta\theta^k\) with Eqs. (19)–(20), and updates \(\theta^{k+1}=\theta^k+\Delta\theta^k\). Here \(k\) [–] is the load-step index, \(q^k\) [N/m] is incremental load, \(\alpha^k\) [–] is its load factor, \(\Delta y_s^k\) [m] controls step size, and \(\beta_\alpha,\beta_\delta\) [–] are algorithmic factors applied to load-factor adjustment and the tolerance \(\beta_\delta\delta\) [m]. The paper does not define numerical values or admissible ranges for \(\alpha^0,\beta_\alpha,\beta_\delta\), and the displayed update ordering is not explained sufficiently to guarantee an independent implementation. ## 6. Constitutive relationship

A **constitutive law** tells us how a material responds locally: for example, how stress follows strain, and what changes irreversibly after yielding. Equilibrium alone says forces balance; it does not say how the material deforms.

Zhang's constitutive picture is an idealized elastoplastic analogy:

- **Inputs/material parameters:** \(E\) [Pa], \(\mu\) [–], imposed \(p\) [Pa], geometry \(b,h,\delta,L\) [m], and load \(q(s)\) [N/m] or section resultants.
- **Kinematic fields:** \(\theta(s)\), curvature \(\theta'(s)\), longitudinal strain \(\varepsilon_s\), and shear strain \(\gamma_{sy}\).
- **Internal/state variables:** \(y_s(s)\), jammed/sliding classification, \(N_J\), \(M_J\), \(K_J\), and loading history through the incremental algorithm.
- **Stress outputs:** \(\sigma(y,s)\) and \(\tau(y,s)\).
- **Structural output:** rotation and deflection/load–deflection response.

In the jammed region, \(|\tau|<\mu p\), behavior is reversible, and the normal bending response uses Young's modulus through \(M=EI_J\kappa\), with curvature \(\kappa=d\theta/ds\). In the sliding region, \(|\tau|=\mu p\), shear stress is capped while shear strain may change; this is the ideal-plastic part. Pressure and friction set the yield stress, the shear resultant sets \(y_s\), and \(y_s\) sets the surviving jammed inertia and therefore bending compliance.

The paper does **not** provide a conventional one-to-one law \(\tau=G\gamma_{sy}\), a hardening law, or an explicit interface-slip displacement evolution law. Its own text says there is no one-to-one shear stress–strain mapping in yielding. Thus “constitutive model” is justified as a yield-rule-plus-elastic-bending construction, not as a complete experimentally identified three-dimensional constitutive tensor. **VERIFIED FULL TEXT — Zhang 2025, Sects. 2 and 4.**

## 7. Treatment of interlayer slip

Zhang does not retain individual interfaces. Slip is represented as a **distributed yielded region** in an equivalent continuous medium:

- \(|y|\le y_s(s)\) is a sliding core at the Coulomb shear cap;
- \(|y|>y_s(s)\) is the jammed elastic part;
- \(y_s(s)\) moves as section shear force changes;
- shear strain \(\gamma_{sy}\) conceptually includes extension and slippage, but the paper does not solve a separate relative-slip displacement field.

This is neither merely a scalar stiffness reduction nor a discrete interface model. The stiffness reduction emerges from the decreasing inertia \(I_J(y_s)\), while the model also gives \(\sigma\) and \(\tau\) fields.

Caruso instead retains the identities of the interfaces. The central interface slips first; subsequent symmetric interface pairs slip outward. Each event changes the effective grouping of layers and produces a new piecewise-linear stiffness segment. Its critical forces \(F_i\) and deflections \(w_i\) carry explicit interface index \(i\) and layer count \(N\). **VERIFIED FULL TEXT — Caruso 2023, Sect. 2 and Fig. 2, PDF pp. 3–4.**

Central distinction: **Caruso predicts which interface slips; Zhang predicts how much of a continuous cross-sectional band is yielded.** Mapping one description into the other quantitatively is not demonstrated in Zhang and remains **UNRESOLVED**.

## 8. Role of layer count \(N\)

Zhang begins from \(h=N\delta\), then takes \(\delta/h\ll1\). The integer \(N\) disappears from most governing equations. Total thickness \(h\) carries the macroscopic size, while \(\delta\) remains in \(Q_{\max}\) and in the saturation \(y_s=h/2-\delta\). Therefore:

- \(N\) is not an explicit state index in the CLJM;
- at fixed \(h\), changing \(N\) changes \(\delta=h/N\), so layer count remains implicit at the full-slip boundary;
- equation count no longer scales with \(N-1\) interfaces;
- the authors state that the continuum assumption works effectively for roughly \(N\gtrsim10\), supported by comparisons involving 10- and 25-layer FEA, but do not conduct a systematic convergence/error study over \(N\).

The plotted continuum solution in Fig. 6 is labeled “50 layers (continuum-based),” while discrete FEA curves use 10 and 25 layers. This plotting label should not be mistaken for a discrete 50-interface CLJM. **VERIFIED FULL TEXT — Zhang 2025, Sect. 2 and Fig. 6, printed pp. 822 and 827 / PDF pp. 2 and 7.**

Claiming that model complexity is fully independent of \(N\) is only partly justified. The analytical architecture is independent of the number of interfaces (**INFERENCE**), but the paper gives no runtime, mesh-convergence, or algorithm-iteration scaling measurements (**UNRESOLVED**).

## 9. Role of vacuum pressure

The supported causal chain is:

\[
\text{applied pressure }p
\longrightarrow \text{assumed normal contact level}
\longrightarrow \tau_{\max}=\mu p
\longrightarrow Q_{\mathrm{slip}},Q_{\max},y_s(Q)
\longrightarrow I_J(y_s)
\longrightarrow \text{rotation/deflection}.
\]

Pressure \(p\) [Pa] appears directly in the Coulomb cap and Eqs. (4)–(9). Equations (5) and (6) make both shear-force thresholds linear in pressure. At a fixed applied \(Q\), raising \(p\) delays or reduces the yielded/sliding region; lowering \(p\) does the opposite. That directional statement is **INFERENCE directly from the equations**.

In full jamming, the elastic bending rigidity \(EI_J=Ebh^3/12\) has no \(p\) term: pressure decides whether the section can remain jammed, not the elastic modulus once it is jammed. This parallels Caruso's verified result that pre-slip stiffness is pressure-independent while transition loads scale with pressure. **VERIFIED FULL TEXT — Zhang Eqs. (5)–(6), (19)–(20); Caruso 2023, Sect. 5.2, PDF p. 6.**

Important limitation: Zhang assumes the contact-related friction capacity is uniform \(\mu p\) and neglects deformation-induced interlaminar normal stress. The FEA comparison then shows that neglected transverse normal stress narrows the real sliding region relative to CLJM. Pressure is therefore an imposed cap parameter, not a solved, spatially varying contact-pressure field. **VERIFIED FULL TEXT — Zhang 2025, Sects. 2 and 5, printed pp. 823 and 828 / PDF pp. 3 and 8.**

## 11. Numerical method / FEM

Finite-element analysis divides a structure into small elements and solves equilibrium and material/contact laws numerically. Here it serves as a higher-detail comparison because a discrete layered mesh can show the stress oscillations that a continuous theory deliberately smooths away.

Zhang's FEA appears explicitly layered in Fig. 6: separate 10- and 25-layer beams are visible, and through-height FEA stress curves fluctuate layer by layer. The simulations use CPS4R plane-stress quadrilaterals. The CLJM is not the FEA material model; it is the orange analytical field against which the discrete FEA results are compared. **VERIFIED FULL TEXT — Zhang 2025, Fig. 6 and Sect. 5, printed pp. 827–828 / PDF pp. 7–8.**

The numerical comparison asks three different questions:

1. Does the continuum normal-stress field \(\sigma(y,s)\) approximate the layered FEA field?
2. Does the continuum shear-stress field \(\tau(y,s)\) approximate it?
3. Does agreement improve away from boundaries and as layer count increases?

At 3 N, Fig. 6a represents full jamming and compares sections near \(s=1\) mm and centrally at \(s=20\) mm. At 4 N, Fig. 6b represents half slipping and compares \(s=10\) and 30 mm. At 5.5 N, Fig. 6c represents full slipping and compares the same general interior locations. The text contains a duplicated “\(s=10\)” phrase for the half-slip sections, while the figure itself labels 10 and 30 mm; the figure labels are used here. **VERIFIED FULL TEXT with source typo — printed pp. 827–828, Fig. 6.**

Results:

- Full-jamming normal and shear fields match closely in an interior section.
- Near \(s=1\) mm, discrete shear oscillations and boundary effects cause departure from the parabolic CLJM profile; the oscillation amplitude decreases and the field becomes smoother as layers increase.
- In half slip, interior profiles are close, but FEA predicts a narrower sliding region because transverse normal stress—omitted from CLJM—raises local friction capacity.
- In full slip, FEA shear fluctuates around the sliding-region prediction, but jammed-region shear differs substantially because the deformed segments act as curved beams while CLJM uses a straight-beam shear pattern.

The continuum and FEA do not demonstrably make the same assumptions. Both use the reported \(E,\mu,p\) and geometry, but the paper does not document the FEA contact law sufficiently to establish exact constitutive equivalence. Nor does it report a norm, percentage error, convergence study, or runtime comparison. Those are **UNRESOLVED**, not negative results.

## 17. Is Zhang's model homogenization?

The terms should be separated:

- **Continuum modeling:** represents material quantities as continuous fields rather than discrete objects.
- **Homogenization:** derives macroscopic equations/properties from microscale structure, normally with an explicit averaging or asymptotic scale-separation argument.
- **Effective-medium modeling:** assigns a continuum response that reproduces selected aggregate behavior of heterogeneous constituents.
- **Equivalent-property modeling:** replaces the structure by scalar or tensor properties such as an equivalent bending stiffness.
- **Reduced-order modeling:** reduces the number of degrees of freedom or state variables needed to predict selected outputs.

Zhang is unambiguously a **continuum-based model**: the paper explicitly takes a thin-layer continuum limit and solves continuous stress fields. It is also a **reduced representation relative to interface-by-interface models**, because a field and one moving boundary replace many discrete slip states. It has an **effective-medium flavor**, since discrete frictional interactions become an elastoplastic continuum yield cap.

It is not merely an equivalent-property model, because it predicts \(\sigma(y,s)\), \(\tau(y,s)\), and \(y_s(s)\), not just one stiffness. However, the paper does not derive averaged effective moduli or a formal microscale-to-macroscale constitutive tensor. The safest statement is:

> Zhang presents a continuum-limit, elastoplastic-like surrogate for a many-layer jamming beam. Calling it “homogenized” is reasonable only informally; “formal homogenization” is not demonstrated.

That classification is **INFERENCE based on VERIFIED FULL-TEXT model structure**, not an author claim.

## 19. My current research direction in simple language

### One sentence

I am testing when a continuum model can safely replace an interface-by-interface model of a many-layer vacuum-jammed beam, and where its predictions break down.

### One paragraph

Caruso can predict layer-by-layer slip but becomes increasingly elaborate as layers are added; Zhang replaces those interfaces with continuous stress fields and a moving slip boundary. My provisional direction is to compare the two descriptions and experiments systematically across layer count, vacuum pressure, bending load or curvature, and slip regime, so that the error and failure boundaries of the continuum approximation are measured rather than assumed. This is a research hypothesis, not yet confirmed novelty.

### Technical version

The provisional project would quantify the domain of validity of continuum-limit representations for vacuum layer-jamming beams by comparing a distributed elastoplastic slip-field model against discrete-interface mechanics, frictional-contact simulation, and controlled bending experiments. Candidate independent variables are \(N\), \(p\), \(\delta/h\), load/curvature, and slip regime; candidate outputs are force–deflection error, stress/slip-front error, transition-load error, and hysteresis error. The intended product is a falsifiable validity/breakdown map, contingent on further literature verification showing that such a map has not already been established.

### Source ambiguities that must not be hidden

1. **Eq. (7):** no explicit full-slip central-core branch is printed for \(|Q|>Q_{\max}\); continuation of the Coulomb cap is inferred from prose.
2. **After Eq. (13):** \(\sigma_S=f(y)\) and the mathematics imply independence from \(s\), while one prose phrase says “depends only on \(s\).”
3. **After Eq. (18):** the printed \(N_J(s_0)=N(s_0)2y_s(s_0)/h\) conflicts with the stated jammed area under uniform axial stress.
4. **Eq. (20):** the second branch appears to omit width \(b\) and is dimensionally m\(^3\), not m\(^4\).
5. **Incremental algorithm:** numerical values/ranges for \(\alpha^0,\beta_\alpha,\beta_\delta\) and the update logic are not sufficiently explained for unambiguous independent implementation.
6. **FEA:** the main text does not specify the interface contact formulation.
7. **Experiment:** repeats, loading rate, parameter-identification method, and numerical error metrics are not reported in the inspected main text.
