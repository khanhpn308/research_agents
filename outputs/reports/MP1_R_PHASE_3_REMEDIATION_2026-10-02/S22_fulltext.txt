                            Chapter 1.              Introduction
•
    1.1. Overview


•   The cable consists of an assemblage of wires laid helically around a core. The cable is

    widely used as an efficient structure for use in cable-stayed structures, for signal

    transmission and for power transmission. For overhead power transmission cables, both
•   strength and dynamic characteristics are critical design parameters. Due to the inherently

    low damping characteristics of long span cables, vibration can result in damage to

•   transmission cables and shorten their useful life. Moreover, for cable-stayed structures,

    vibrations can lead to a decrease in the cable load-carrying capacity.       In addition,

    agreement on helical cable designs is difficult.    Cable design choices include jacket
•   materials, water blocking techniques, and the number of fibers to place within the cable.

    The cable design chosen depends on the cable's intended application. Each different


•   application may require a slightly different cable design. The cable must meet minimal

    levels of performance in safety and durability (able to withstand shock, vibration, etc.).

    These current design concerns suggest that the dynamic behavior of cables need to be

•   better understood.



    Determination of various mechanical characteristics of helical cables has never been an
•   easy task. Substantial work has been accomplished over the past twenty years to model

    the structural behavior of such cables. The most important dynamical property of cables


•   is wire slippage that creates large variations in flexural rigidity.    Various flexural

    rigidities have been predicted by a number of previously developed models. In addition,



•                                               1
    the static and dynamic behavior of cables also have been predicted by previously reported

I   models.



    The main focus of recent research has been the response of the cable under dynamic

•   loading conditions. The primary dynamic response of cables includes free vibration and

    forced vibration.


•   Gutzer, Seemann and Hagedorn (1995) applied a MASING model for a steel cable

    damping vibration system, in which the cable was analyzed as a collection of discrete and

•   continuous systems of bonded layers. Each of the different layers consists of one or more

    parallel JENKIN or PRANDTL elements. The stick-slip behavior between the structural

    parts was simulated as a special case of static hysteresis. The slowly varying amplitude
•   method and the phase method were used in the simulation to identify the damping

    parameters of the physical cable.


•   Gatti-Bono and Perkins (2003) conducted an analysis of a two-dimensional towed cable

    in an attempt to cover all aspects of cable under tension, torsion, and two-axis bending.

•   A numerical algorithm based on separate finite differencing in time and in space is

    described.   Seabed contact forces are added by modeling the seabed as an elastic


•   foundation with a prescribed profile. In deriving the motion of the cable, the cable was

    treated as a one-dimensional dynamic elastic inextensible continuum.




•

•                                              2
•
    An alternative approach of cable analysis was suggested by Zhong (2003). The cable is


•   modeled as a one-dimensional continuum with varying flexural rigidity. A simplified

    cable vibration model, the frictional bending model (FBM), was developed to estimate

    cable damping due to internal friction. Her study showed that the numerical solution can

•   be derived by directly integrating the equation of motion of the cable. The results of her

    study showed some agreement with experimental data.


•   All of these investigations address the improvements of dynamic analyses of the cable,

    and that the effects of internal dry friction between helical wire layers of a cable playa

•   significant role in the dynamic behavior of the cable. Although these research studies

    illustrate different applications of the cable, the major focus has been the dynamic

    response of these cables to external load and internal frictions. Their results provide
•   methods for cable dynamic analyses, and are very helpful for the design of cable systems.




•   Some of these analyses have resulted in rather complex stiffness matrixes, while others

    have focused on individual wires of cable layers, which may not be easily applied to

    other types of cables.

•
    The objective of this study is to develop a mathematical cable model to predict cable


•   vibration and the attendant reduction in vibration amplitude due to internal friction

    caused by relative wire slippage, which allows a faster identification of the vibration

    nature of the cable. In addition, a computer program is to be developed to investigate

•

•                                               3
•
    cable free vibration cases using the mathematical cable model. Finally, the validity of


•   this model is to be verified by experimental data for forced vibration.




    1.2. Problem Statement
•
    The flexible cable is complex structure made of multiple layers of helically-served and


•   axial components with dissimilar materials. The cable can have a nonlinear structural

    characteristic where deformations are large, materials have nonlinear stress-strain curves

    and frictional sliding of helical wires occrues. Consequently, the governing equation of

•   motion of a cable can be a highly nonlinear. Because the dynamic behavior of cable is

    not fully understood, especially when the loads applied on the cable are a combination of

    tension, torsion and compression, the analytical solution can be impractical to achieve.
•   Therefore, numerical methods have to be employed.




•   This study uses the flexural rigidity-curvature relationship predicted by CableCAD®

    (2003) into the equation of motion of the cable. In this study, the motion of the cable is

    simulated by applying variational theory and using a fifth-order Hermite interpolation

•   function (Ross, 1998). The numerical solution of the equation of motion of cable is

    achieved by using Newmark's Method (Stein, 2002) with linear acceleration.

    3.2. Frictional Bending Model

•
    To solve the nonlinear equation of motion (EI is a discontinuous function of cable

    curvature), a numerical method was applied. Due to wire slippage between adjacent

•   layers, modeling the cable becomes much more difficult.




•      3.2.1 Cable Components


    As shown in Figures 3.1 and 3.2, the typical cable comprises several layers, each layer

•   having its own material and geometrical properties. Layer frictional interactions strongly

    affect the properties of the entire cable. The cable used in this research consists of elastic,

    high modulus, high strength steel wires. When tensioned and bent, helical wires can slip
•   relative to wires in adjacent layers resulting in energy dissipation.

       3.2.2 Bending Behavior of Cable Layers
•
    For a cable with small curvature and suffcient applied tension, the shear forces between


•   layers and wires are relatively small in magnitude and can not overcome the friction

    between layers.   Therefore, sliding will not occur, and energy will not be dissipated

    during the bending cycle. In this case, the bending moment-curvature relationship is

•   linear and the assumption of plane-sections remaining plane is observed.




•   If the cable is preformed when manufactured so that large radial pressures are developed

    between layers, internal frictional forces can be developed without the application of

    tension. By controlling the degree of perform as a design parameter, the desired degree

•   of internal frication can be developed to dissipate energy from the vibrating cable.

       3.3.2 Methods of Direct Integration
•
    There are many methods for advancing the solution ofEq. (3.20) in time. Essentially, the


•   problem has been reduced to solving a set of coupled ordinary differential equations. For

    each time step, the following procedures occur for each element. Given the general nodal

    displacements, velocities, and acting forces in time, T, sum the external forces and use


•   the resultant forces acting on the masses at the nodes to predict accelerations at the nodes.

    From the displacements, velocities, and accelerations at time, t, and at a time step, a new

    set of general nodal displacements can be found.

•
    The algorithms commonly used can be categorized into two types, explicit methods and


•   implicit methods.    Explicit methods, which predict the next state using the element

    matrices, do not require a large set of equations to be solved at each time interval. The

    equations for one element only calculate forces at neighboring nodes explicitly connected

•   to a node given a displacement. If in one time step, only these immediate neighbors are




                                                 25
•
•
    affected, then the information in the element matrices is enough to predict the evolution


•   of the solution.    The explicit methods cope well with nonlinearities; eg., plastic

    deformation. However, the explicit methods provide unstable solutions if time steps used

    are too large.

•
    On the other hand, implicit methods permit a much larger time step, but introduce


•   artificial damping, and require a pseudo-static problem (including inertia loading) to be

    solved at each time interval. In structural dynamic problems implicit integration schemes

    give acceptable solutions with time steps usually one or two orders of magnitude larger

•   than the stability limit of explicit methods.       If the time step is too large, higher

    frequencies in the response will be filtered out.

    4.4. Discussion

•
    A simplified cable vibration model has been developed to estimate cable vibration

    considering internal friction. Instead of adding an extra extend damping device, variable

•   flexure rigidity was used in the equation of motion of the cable.           The software

    CableCAD® (2003) was used to derive the relationship between flexural rigidity and


•   curvature of the deformed cable configurations.




•

•                                               46
•
       4.4.1 Error Analysis

•
    The solution follows an incremental iterative scheme for physical and numerical reasons.

    In this case, there are three main sources of errors to be estimated.
•
    First, the stiffness matrix simulates the cable dynamical propriety, but it might not be the


•   same as the actual stiffness matrix of a real cable. For example, the cable's EI curve

    shows as many jumps as the number of wire layers that slip. When the cable passes

    through a jump, its dynamic propriety changes rapidly. In addition, the stiffness matrix

•   in this study focuses on the bending behavior of the cable, and does not cover the

    proprieties of the cable undergoing the torsion and tension.


•   Secondly, there are some scratches and dents on the surfaces of cable specimens tested

    for interlayer friction (see Appendix A). They are caused during cable manufacture and

•   result in measurement error.



    Thirdly, there might be an accumulation of errors during the equation solving process.

•   Because the length of each element must not be smaller than the longest half-lay length,

    which is a relatively large element length, it may cause errors during the finite element


•   approach. Before iteration, least square curve fitting technique is applied and forms

    smooth curves for cable configuration. Table 4.1 shows the sum of the squared error

    after the first iteration for the simply supported case. Furthermore, EI of some elements

•   are fixed, for example, in the simply supported case, flexural rigidities of the first and last




•                                                 47
•
    element are fixed to be the constant because there no moments at the two end-points,


•   which may not reflect the real conditions of the elements. Due to the reasons stated

    above, errors occur in each equation solutions. During the process of time stepping

    iterations, errors accumulate with each time step.    Subsequently, it causes relatively

•   significant errors in the final solutions.

                             Chapter 6.              Conclusions
•
•   6.1. Objectives Achieved



    In the this study, the cable is considered as a system of helical wires and a core with

•   distributed dry friction forces at their interfaces and modeled as a one-dimensional

    continuum, where the wires are not being modeled individually.


•   The simplified equation of motion governing the cable vibration is derived, which is a

    nonlinear, fourth order partial differential equation with respect to the span of the cable

•   and the second order derivative with respect to time. The finite element method (FEM) is

    applied to solve this equation. The partial differential equation was discretized into time

    and space domains.
•
    This model also is used to analyze cable vibration. An implicit expression for predicting


•   the next state using the elemental matrices is derived. Numerical examples are given to

    show the response history.




•   Since researchers have been studying the cable dynamic behavior, many models have

    been developed with different approaches but there was not a unified model that could be

    used by all manufacturers and engineers. The model established in this study can be
•
•                                               56
•
    applied to any kind of cable assuming the relationship between cable flexural rigidity and


•   cable curvature is known.




•   6.2. Future Study



    For future studies, an analytical bending model of cable might be extended to include the
•   effects of torsion, axial elongation, interlayer pressures, and both of material nonlinearity

    and geometric nonlinearity. A new stiffness matrix may be constructed based on those


•   effects.



    The determination of interlayer pressure would require more accurate experimental work.

•   Pressure indicating sensor films could be used to obtain a more accurate measurement of

    the interlayer pressure.


•   The effect of internal damping also may be interesting for future studies. Also, in order

    to include the external damping effect, the damped vibration of cable should be

•   formulated.



    The current model is based on the small deformation assumption. In order to obtain more

•   realistic results, the large motion theory should be applied.

    A.3. Limitation of Experiment

•
    There are some scratches and dents on the surface of cable specimens (shown in Figure

    A.IO and A.II).      These scratches and dents were produced when the cable was
•   manufactured and they cause some errors in the interlayer pressure measurements. The

    combination of these scratches and dents and the different lay lengths of each layer gives

•   the wave-like frictional force history and the sudden change on the curves, which also

    cause some difficulties in the determination of interlayer pressure. Taking the production

    of outside diameter and the length of cable specimen as the contact area for interlayer
•   pressure computation also is a source of error.
