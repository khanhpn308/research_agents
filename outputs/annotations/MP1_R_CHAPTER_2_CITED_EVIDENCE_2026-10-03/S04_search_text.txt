B5: Abstract—A ﬂexible surgical robot that can adjust its stiffness
guarantees safe operation by satisfying both the high ﬂexibility
required when the robot approaches a surgical target and the
more stiffness necessary for the end effector to perform surgical
procedures. Therefore, this paper proposes a ﬂexible surgical robot
that consists of a central backbone, eight super-elastic wires as
peripheral backbones, SMA springs, rubber tubes, and several
disks. The inner diameter of the SMA springs that can be changed
via their temperature creates a tightening force change to apply
onto the eight backbone wires of the robot to adjust the overall
stiffness of the robot. The simple structure is favorable for robot
miniaturization. Frictional force and stiffness modulation experi-
ments of the robot are performed. The results conﬁrmed that the
robot’s stiffness was increased approximately 1.4 times between
the martensite state and the austenite state of the SMA spring. The
relationship model between the ﬂexural rigidity of the robot and
the frictional force between the SMA spring with the peripheral
backbones is established. We compared the ﬂexural rigidity values
obtained from our model with those of the experimental result and
conﬁrmed that the model was valid.
B6: Index Terms—Laparoscopy, soft robot materials and design,
tendon/wire, variable stiffness.

because it can be bent inside the human body, unlike long and
rigid surgical tools. SPL using a ﬂexible surgical robot has the
advantage of providing patients with a small scar, little pain, and
short-term recovery, although SPL still has some drawbacks,
such as the cost of the instruments, the length of the operation,
and the surgeon’s operational complexity. Considering the re-
quired diameter, length, and end effector force for each surgical
ﬁeld, ﬂexible surgical robots have been researched in vari-
ous surgical ﬁelds, including otolaryngology surgery [1], brain
surgery [2], [3], cardiovascular surgery [4]–[6], gastrointestinal
surgery [7], [8], and natural oriﬁce transluminal endoscopic
surgery [9]–[12]. To perform robotic surgery with a ﬂexible
robot in a limited ﬁeld of view, it is necessary to sense the
robot shape and track the position of the robot’s tip. However,
the greater DOF in a ﬂexible robot, the more difﬁcult it is to
sense the robot shape and precise control. Therefore, to improve
the control stability of a ﬂexible robot, medical imaging sys-
tems such as computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance
imaging (MRI), and ultrasound (US) have been used for robotic
surgery. In particular, MRI has a high resolution and ensures
patient safety, while US has a low resolution, and CT has a
problem of allergic reactions and organ damage in patients
becauseofcontrastagentsandradiation.InanMRIenvironment,
the materials of a ﬂexible surgical robot must be compatible with
an electromagnetic ﬁeld. However, most metals cause noise in
MRI and are not suitable for ﬂexible surgical robot materials.
Some materials with MRI compatibility, such as brass [13],
shape memory alloys (SMAs) [14]–[17], silicone rubber [18],
and plastics [15], have been used for ﬂexible surgical robots
with MRI guidance. Among them, SMA is a biocompatible
material used in many surgical robots in MRI environments. An
SMA also has a high-power density. Thus, it is used to create
compact actuators for miniaturization and weight reduction
in surgical robots or grippers. Kim et al. developed an SMA
spring-based active stiffness continuum robot for neurosurgery
and conﬁrmed its MRI compatibility [14]. The high ﬂexibility
of the robot allows it to move smoothly without any injury to
a lesion. However, its stability might be reduced because low
stiffness could bring about undesirable deformation or bending.
In addition, the insufﬁcient stiffness of the robot limits the end
effectorforce,whichisrequiredwhentherobotperformsvarious
operations, such as incision and suturing. As a result, it is nec-
essary to control the stiffness of the robot based on the speciﬁc
scenario.

B1: Many studies have been conducted to realize stiffness con-
trol. Cianchetti et al. proposed a manipulator for minimal ac-
cess surgery that can implement selective stiffness changes by
combining a ﬂuid actuator to enable omnidirectional bending,
elongation functionality, and jamming [18], [19]. Jiang et al.
proposed a high DOF variable stiffness mechanism that can be
highly refracted. The rigidity of each joint can be independently
controlled using the jamming principle through vacuum gener-
ation, and the effect of vacuum pressure and particle type on
stiffness adjustment can be studied [20]. Kim et al. proposed a
stiffness conversion mechanism layer jamming through friction
between the layers of a snake-shaped hollow manipulator com-
posed of several layers of thin mylar ﬁlm and tested the stiffness
andfracturestrengthofthelayerjamming[21],[22].Moses etal.
[23] proposed a stiffness control manipulator whose main body
includes thirty interlocking ﬁbers and a ﬂexible sheath around
the manipulator. The ﬁbers are free to slide along the ﬂexible
sheath that may be evacuated to increase its stiffness. Although
granular and layer jamming approaches achieve signiﬁcant stiff-
ness, the system still requires a large volume or layered structure
to achieve adequate stiffness. Kim et al. proposed a mechanism
to adjust the joint stiffness of a robot with a thin tubular structure
through a unique asymmetric arrangement between the tendons
and the links and combined this with a change in wire tension
[24]. This structure approaches have the advantage of being able
to switch between ﬂexible and stiff modes quickly. However,
the tendons’ tension must be high, and the links must be strong
enough to ensure enough friction to keep the rigidity. Kim et al.
proposed a mechanism to control the stiffness by overlapping
the segment of the inner tube and the segment of the outer tube
through relative translational and rotational movements between
patterned coaxial tubes [25]. Even though its stiffness can be
changed quickly, the robot may be twisted when the tubes are
rotated to change their stiffness. Le et al. proposed a manipulator
consisting of PET and stainless-steel sheaths that controlled the
rigidity through heating, and the rigidity increased by at least
nine times [26]. When heated to 70 °C or higher, the PET will
produce toxic pyrolysis products. Jiang et al. [27] presented a
variable stiffness for continuum manipulator using an SMA-
based sheath. However, the restoring force of the SMA-based
sheath will be disturbed the bending motion of the robot, and
the mesh surface of the sheath will not be arranged regularly and
uniformly on the mesh surface when this structure is bent.
B2: As a result, this paper proposes a stiffness control mecha-
nism for a multi-backbone snake-like robot based on an SMA
spring to address these limitations. The proposed robot has
three characteristics. First, the robot has a relatively simple
structure compared to previous studies, which includes an SMA
spring inserted into a robot composed of several disks, a cen-
tral backbone, a rubber tube, and eight super-elastic wires as
peripheral backbones. Second, the SMA spring is used as an
actuator that applies a strong tightening force to the peripheral
backbones when a current is applied. Third, the core principle
of this stiffness control system is the frictional force between
the rubber layer and the peripheral backbones generated by
the tightening force of the SMA spring. The stiffness control
mechanism produces sufﬁcient stiffness and stiffness range
within a simple structure. The stiffness of the robot will be
modeled using the energy equation containing frictional work,
stored spring energy, and work of an external force applied to
the robot. In Section II, we discuss the design of the proposed
robot. We present the stiffness modeling to verify the effect of
friction on the stiffness in Section III. In Section IV, friction and

B5: II. DESIGN AND WORKING PRINCIPLE OF A SURGICAL ROBOT
B6: A. Design and Working Principle
B7: Our surgical robot was designed based on the multi-backbone
snake-like robot [28] proposed by Simaan et al. The surgical
robot consists of three segments, including four disks, a central
backbone, four tendons, and several wires as peripheral back-
bones, as shown in Fig. 1(a). The central backbone is attached
to the center of all disks to keep a constant distance between the
disks and prevent rotation of disks. The wires at the peripheral
backbones are only linked to the base disk so that they can
slide freely through the other disks when the robot is bent.
In Fig. 1(b), each disk has four tendon holes, one hole for the
central backbone and several holes for the peripheral backbone
wires. A rubber tube covers the center segment of the peripheral
backbones and the SMA spring is wound on the outside of the
rubber tube. A current is applied to the SMA spring through
conductive threads attached to both ends to raise its temperature,
as shown in Fig. 1(c).
B8: An SMA has different lattice structures and phases depending
on its temperature. It includes a martensite phase with a face-
centered cubic crystal structure at a low temperature and an
austenite phase with a body-centered cubic crystal structure at a
high temperature. When the SMA is deformed in the martensite
phase, the structure of the crystal lattice is deformed, but the
bonds between atoms are not broken. The structure of the crystal
lattice is transformed from twinned martensite to detwinned
martensite. After that, as the temperature of the SMA increases,
the detwinned martensite changes to austenite lattice structure.
As a result, the SMA is restored to its original shape. As the

to the twinned martensite, and the restoring force is removed. In
other words, the SMA can restore its memorized shape through
phase change based on temperature. In our mechanism, the inner
diameter (D0) of the SMA spring, which is determined when it
is trained for its memorized shape, is smaller than the diameter
of the rubber tube (Drb) covering the peripheral backbones, so
that the SMA spring applies a tightening force to the rubber tube
and peripheral backbones. The SMA spring in a fully martensite
phaseisexpandedintheradialdirectionbytherobotbodyhaving
a relatively large diameter (Df), as shown in Fig. 1(d). When the
phase of the SMA changes to the full austenite phase through
heating, it generates a tightening force on the rubber tube in the
radial direction to restore the original smaller diameter (D0 <
Df = Drb), as shown in Fig. 1(e). This force (FT) will tighten
the rubber tube to increase the frictional force (Ff) between the
rubber tube and peripheral backbones. This tightening force
can be changed depending on the phase of the SMA spring.
When the phase of the SMA spring changes into martensite
through natural cooling, the force starts to decrease, and the
SMA spring has a relatively weak tightening force on the rubber
tube to reduce the frictional force. The core of the proposed
stiffness control mechanism is the frictional force generated
through the tightening force between the SMA spring and the
rubber tube. The frictional force increases the robot stiffness
by restraining the movement of the peripheral backbones that
could relatively freely slide with the rubber and disks as the
robot bends, as shown in Fig. 1(f). The diameter of the rubber
tube (the outer diameter of the peripheral backbones) is also an
important variable affecting the tightening force. As mentioned,
the tightening force can be adjusted based on the phase change
of the SMA spring. Therefore, the stiffness of the robot can be
controlled based on the phase of the SMA spring.
B2: Two pairs of opposing tendons embedded in the disks of the
ﬂexible surgical robot can control its bending in two directions.
The tendons are ﬁxed to only the end disk, and they can move
freely on the other disks. When one of the tendon pairs is
pulled, the space between the disks along that tendon decreases,
while the distance between the disks along the opposing tendon
increases. As a result, the ﬂexible surgical robot is bent in this
direction, as shown in Fig. 1(f). Similarly, the ﬂexible surgical
robot is bent in the second direction perpendicular to the ﬁrst
via another pair of tendons.

number of coils of the SMA spring is an important variable that
affects the stiffness control capability of the robot. However,
an SMA spring with many turns complicates the modeling by
causing undesired deformation in the peripheral backbones.
Therefore, in this paper, experiments and analysis were per-
formed using an SMA spring with 1.5 turns to exclude modeling
errors caused by the deformation of the peripheral backbones.
Finally, we performed a preliminary test of the bending capacity
of the robot. In Fig. 2(b), driving the robot using the polyethylene
thread as a tendon proved that the robot can be bent up to 90° in
the vertical, left, and right directions.

backbones affects the tightening force of the SMA spring. There-
fore, this study will investigate the stiffness of a ﬂexible surgical
robot with three different outer diameters of the peripheral
backbones (Dt) of 5.5, 5.75, and 6 mm in Section IV.

B10: A. Friction Force Characterization
B11: The friction force between the rubber tube and the eight
wires relates to the clamping force of the SMA spring. The

B6: V. DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION
B7: In this study, we developed a ﬂexible surgical robot whose
stiffness can be controlled to prevent a lack of stability due
to insufﬁcient stiffness of the robot when used in single-port
laparoscopy. The simple structure of the robot includes a central
backbone, several acrylic disks, eight super-elastic wires, SMA
springs, andarubber tube. TheSMAspringthat wraps therubber
tube generates a tightening force on the rubber to increase the
frictional force. This frictional force restrains the movement of
the super-elastic wires to increase the stiffness of the robot. The
tightening force of the SMA spring can be changed with the
change phase effect caused by a temperature change (martensite
and austenite states).
B8: In this paper, friction and stiffness characterization experi-
ments were performed for SMA springs at two states: martensite
and austenite. To verify the effect of the backbone diameter
on the tightening force, we also conducted experiments with
peripheral backbone diameters of 5.5, 5.75, and 6 mm. The SMA
spring that we use for the experiment is 5.5 mm inner diameter
with a coil of 1.5 turns. The experimental results show that the
robot in the austenite state has a stiffness equal to 1.45, 1.50, and
1.41 times that in the martensite state for backbone diameters
of 5.5 mm, 5.75 mm, and 6 mm, respectively. Compared to
jamming and other methods [22], [29], our mechanism does
not show a signiﬁcant stiffness ratio. However, our proposed
robot has achieved a 150% range in stiffness ratio within a
small structure. The friction and stiffness of the robot tended
to increase as the peripheral backbone diameter increased. In
Figs. 4 and 5, there is more noise in the friction force and force to
bend the robot in the austenite state of the SMA spring because of
increased tightening force and the effect of the dynamic friction
state. However, the noise does not affect the stiffness of the robot.
We also derived the modeled ﬂexural rigidity through the energy
equation for work done by the friction and external force. The
modeled ﬂexural rigidity and the experimental ﬂexural rigidity
were compared to evaluate the validity of the modeling. Since
the errors between the modeled value and the experimental value
were 3.5%, 3.1%, and 5.4%, the modeled ﬂexural rigidity that
we propose was reliable.
B9: This study has the following limitations: (1) the SMA spring
is cooled ambiently, so the cooling time to change from the
austenite to martensite state is long (approximately 60 s); (2)
the robot does not have a temperature control system to maintain
the stiffness in real-time; (3) the stiffness ratio between the rigid
and ﬂexible states is still small; and (4) the difference in ﬂexural
stiffness between the model and the experimental result was sig-
niﬁcant (5.4%). Moreover, we assumed the bending of the wires
of the peripheral backbone was the arc of a circle. As a result,
the total sliding length of wires of the peripheral backbone cal-
culated using (5) may have a larger error as the robot bend more.
In addition, the work done by friction in the friction experiment
is used to calculate the modeled ﬂexural stiffness. In reality, this
friction will have a minor change in the experiments in the same
the SMA spring state (martensite or austenite). In future work,
a temperature control system can be embedded in the robot. We
will increase the number of segments and the number of SMA
springs on the peripheral backbone and combine it with a ﬂuidics
system to increase the friction range to improve the stiffness
ratio. The ﬂuidic system will be used to cool the SMA spring
quickly.
