B8: A B S T R A C T
B9: A new mechanical formulation is presented for assembling the constitutive relationships of single- and multiple-
strand wire ropes. The model aims to predict the lower and upper bounds of the bending stiffness associated to
the limit behaviors of perfect stick and full slip between the constituting wires. The wire rope is described as
a shear deformable beam for which the generalized constitutive equations, accounting for the wires’ geometry
and material properties, are derived according to Saint-Venant’s theory extended to curvilinear thin rods. The
distinguished feature of wire ropes, i.e., the possibility of relative sliding between the wires, is accounted for by
the introduction of eigenstrains in the wires’ strain measures. An experimental campaign is carried out on two
series of short steel wire ropes subjected to quasi-static bending loops obtaining hysteresis cycles for different
amplitudes and rope lengths. The model is validated by comparing theoretically predicted and experimentally
measured stiffnesses obtained from the bending hysteresis cycles as well as from axial-torsional tests present
in the literature.
B10: 1. Introduction
B11: Wire ropes find countless applications in engineering and are em-
ployed in a formidable variety of devices [1–6]. Although the main
property of these structural elements is their ability to withstand high
tensile forces, the idea of taking advantage of the wire ropes capability
to dissipate energy through flexural and torsional mechanisms has
become popular in the last century. The purpose is to exploit the
internal frictional forces resulting from the relative motion that arises
between the wires when the rope deforms to dissipate energy.
B12: The precise understanding of the structural response of wire ropes is
of undeniable industrial interest, with significant implications for vari-
ous applications. However, the description of the mechanical behavior
of these elements is a hard task, as testified by the large scientific liter-
ature on the subject. Until the 21st century, the dissipative properties
of ropes were studied by representing the relationship between hys-
teretic force and displacement through parameters of limited mechan-
ical significance [7–14]. Moreover, these mathematical models always
require an experimental data regarding force–displacement histories for
calibration.
B13: Recent studies on the mechanical response of wire ropes unfold
along two main lines of approach. The first one, developed in recent
years, aims to give a detailed representation of the dissipative behavior
of wire ropes through the description of the actual internal geometry.
This requires the analysis of dissipative forces arising at the wires’

B18: Concerning modeling strategies, a first important reference is the
book of Costello [15] where each wire is modeled as a spatial
Kirchhoff–Love-Clebsch thin rod (for reference, see [16]) with no

a well-known technique in continuum mechanics that is often used
for modeling systems of numerous discrete elements with repetitive
structure as a continuum. This method was first applied to cable
modeling by Hobbs and Raoof [17]. In this model, the classical twisted
rod theory for the behavior of helical wires has been extended to
include a set of kinematic compatibility conditions. The individual layer
of wires is replaced by an equivalent cylindrical orthotropic sheet,
which is assumed to be thin and to be in a plane stress state. Another

later extended by Jolicoeur and Cardou [19,20]. Also in these models,
each layer is replaced with a cylinder of transversely isotropic material
and elastic constants can be used as free parameters to be fitted or
else estimated rationally from contact mechanics equations as in the
case of the orthotropic sheet model. Hoppe presented a model [21] in
which the structure and the components are assumed to be subjected to
pure tensile forces, the bending and torsional stiffness for both of them
being neglected. Contact and friction between the components are also
neglected, but such an approximation is justified for monotonic axial
loading. It should be noted that this analytical model provides only the
overall tensile behavior. Leech et al. [22], presented a more complex
quasi-static analysis of fibre ropes and included it in a commercial
software: fibre rope modeller (FRM). Their analysis is based on the prin-
ciple of virtual work and can take frictional effects into account. The
program expresses tension and torque as functions of elongation and
twist. Leech’s model appears to be very sophisticated, with an accurate
mechanical modeling of the components of the fibre ropes behavior
and their interactions. However, only axial and torsional actions are
taken into account. Kumar et al. [23] extended Costello’s model to
obtain analytical expressions for maximum contact stresses induced
in the strands with a metallic core. Huang [24] studied the contact
mode conditions for a seven wire strand. Jolicoeur [25] compared the
results obtained by several mathematical models of a seven wire strand,
specifically chosen with a geometry to explain the different contact
modes. The literature on the effects of interfacial contacts is limited.
Although a few studies have addressed the effects of friction at the
interfaces, the results show significant dispersion, since the parame-
ters considered vary from author to author. The effects of tangential
distributed forces at the contact surfaces are seldom considered. These
forces cause the wires to slip on the core and hence can change the
individual wire as well as the overall strand response. A mathematical
model to represent the effects of tangential and normal distributed
forces in a coupled contact was formulated by Parthasarathy [26].
Gnanavel et al. [27] presented an analytical model explaining the
importance of the interfacial loads and their effects in coupled contact.
They identified the threshold limit at which the contact mode changes
from a coupled arrangement to the arrangement of core-wire contact.
Ghoreishi et al. [28] developed a nonlinear elastic continuum model
for the analysis of the overall axial stiffness of fibrous structures with
a large number of twisted components. By contrast with multilayered
approaches, the structure under consideration is herein depicted as a
set of coaxial helices only characterized by their external lay angle and
corresponding radius. The contact and friction forces are neglected thus
no consideration about the energy dissipation is present in their work.
Gnanavel and Parthasarathy [29] presented a mathematical model for
the static behavior of a seven wire strand subjected to an axial tensile
load and twisting action. The mathematical model takes into account
the Poisson-effect in the wires, inter-wire pressure and the effects of
friction between the core and the helical wires to the strand stiffness
coefficients. Sauter and Hagedorn [30] employed distributed Jenkin
elements to model the damper cable of a Stockbridge damper [31].
The damper impedances was simulated using data obtained from a
simple experiment carried out with a piece of damper cable. In this
study the Masing model has been generalized for a continuous system
such as a cable. Gerges [32] conducted an experimental study dealing
with wire rope springs deforming in the tension-compression mode.
The authors developed a semi-analytical model that depends entirely
on the dimensions and properties of the springs. This model describes
the restoring force as a function of the displacement and the velocity
sign only. The restoring force was modeled as a direct summation of
two components: an elastic force and a damping force. The nonlinear
non-hysteretic elastic force was based on an equivalent cross-section
having the same area and moment of inertia of the wire rope. The large
displacement analysis was carried out using a consistent curved beam
element. The nonlinear hysteretic damping force was modeled as a
B2: function of the elastic force and the velocity sign and the damping force
constants were empirically related to the dimensions of the springs.
The model agreed well with the experimental results. Sun et al. [33]
developed an elasto-plastic 3D contact problem of a strand using FE
analysis. In this work the augmented Lagrangian multiplier method has
been used to handle friction conditions between the centre wire and the
helical wire. The analyses were carried out by assigning displacements
as boundary conditions and, despite the constant forces are described
accurately, no hysteresis curves are presented. Ma et al. [34] developed
a model for several types of wire ropes with metal cores and built a geo-
metric model for the 6 × 19 IWS wire rope. A 3D finite element model
for calculating the deformation of wire rope was set up. Completely
constraining one end of the wire rope and applying an axial force to the
other, the authors numerically simulated the stresses and deformations
of the wire, obtaining the deformation distribution of each wire within
the wire rope under different laying directions. However no friction was
taken into account, hence, hysteresis curves are not discussed. Another
3D finite element approach, which considers friction and geometric
nonlinearities, is described in [35], where the hysteretic behavior of
a short steel strand subjected to coupled tensile-bending cycles was
obtained. However, the authors highlighted the computational burden
and noted that while the model provided a good qualitative description,
it fell short in providing a quantitative match with experimental results.
B3: The Kirchhoff–Love theory [16] for thin curvilinear beams is often
used as a basic model to study the mechanical behavior of wire ropes
subjected to tension and torsion. This approach allows to evaluate the
stress state in each wire and to predict the general behavior of the
rope. However, the stress state of the wires is derived with simplifying
assumptions about internal forces distribution, often using differential
equations of motion for curved beams thus ignoring the real distri-
bution of contact regions, which are generally discrete. The following
approaches explicitly account for the superhelix (doubly-twisted helix)
configuration of independent wires for multi-stranded ropes. Elata
et al. [36] set two limit kinematic hypotheses that emulate a well
lubricated rope and infinite friction between adjacent wires equivalent
to what is now termed as ‘full slip’ and ‘perfect stick’ states. Comparison
with experimental data in terms of stiffnesses showed general agree-
ment with the model’s predictions, even though the coefficients are
somewhat higher than those measured experimentally. Usabiaga and
Pagalday [37] set a new wire kinematics based again on the assumption
that friction between wires is high enough to prevent wire-sliding be-
tween adjacent wires in the strands, while Xiang et al. [38] developed
the model under the assumption of absence of friction between the
wires. Fraldi et al. [39] investigated the tension-torsion strength and
stiffness of helically arranged bundles, observing that the different cross
sections and helical angles of the layers determine a non-homogeneous
stress redistribution when one or more wires break.
B4: With the aim of exploiting the dissipative capabilities of wire ropes,
the bending problem of the strand is also addressed in the literature.
When a strand is bent, internal frictional forces arise and initially tend
to counteract the relative sliding of the strands. When a limit deflection
is reached, these forces are overcome by the difference in normal forces
between adjacent wires, and this causes relative sliding. As already
mentioned, this phenomenon constitutes a dissipative mechanism ex-
ploited in applications and gives rise to the experimentally observable
hysteretic behavior of ropes subjected to bending. Concerning the
bending behavior, a first major contribution is the work of Papail-
iou [40], who developed a mechanical model for strands subjected to
simultaneous tensile and bending loads and obtains the maximum and
minimum stiffnesses.
B5: Foti and Martinelli, inspired by the previous ideas, developed a
simplified beam model for single strands [41] under tension and
bending. With a particular focus on bending behavior, one of the aims
of the model is to provide estimates of the maximum and minimum
stiffnesses exhibited by wire ropes undergoing hysteresis cycles. The
authors provide a complete and formal derivation of the expressions

B2: Carboni et al. [46,47] employed a phenomenological approach in
which they generalized the Bouc-Wen model to identify the hysteresis
cycles exhibited by short wire ropes made of steel and NiTiNOL under
coupled tension-bending loadings. These models provide an accurate
description of hysteretic softening and hardening behaviors resulting
from the interaction of geometric, frictional and material nonlinearities,
and they can be suitably applied for both static and dynamic prob-
lems [48,49]. However, according to the phenomenological nature, a
direct correlation with the mechanical and geometrical properties of
the wire ropes is not established. Furthermore, Carboni et al. [50]
proposed a semi-phenomenological model consisting of a planar shear-
deformable beam equipped with material and geometric nonlinearities.
Specifically, a Bouc-Wen relationship between bending moment and
curvature was implemented to capture the loading-unloading hysteresis
cycles, while a fraction of the circular area that encloses the wire ropes’
section was assumed to estimate the elastic stiffness.
B3: The present work, taking inspiration from previous achievements,
proposes a mechanical model providing robust estimates of the maxi-
mum and minimum stiffness for short ropes in bending as well as under
axial-torsional loading. For the purposes of this research, a short rope is
defined as a rope element, having a ratio of cross-sectional diameter to
length between 1∕12 and 1∕5, whose bending stiffness dominates the
mechanical response in terms of bending moments and shear forces.
One of the novelties of the model is the capability of handling multi-
strands as well as simple-strand ropes. The elastic problem for the beam
is solved analytically and the explicit expressions for the displacements
are given in terms of the beam stiffness matrix coefficients. The elastic-
ity matrix for the beam is obtained starting from the internal structure
and the geometric and material properties of single wires.
B4: The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 illustrates the mechan-
ical model developed for interpreting the structural response of wire
ropes in the linear regime. Section 3 presents the experimental setup
and post-processing of the results in terms of maximum and minimum
stiffness attained in the hysteresis cycles by short ropes subjected to
pure bending. Finally, Section 4 and Section 5 illustrate the analytical
solutions of the boundary value problems and the comparisons of
the theoretical maximum and minimum stiffnesses with experimental
results for the bending tests performed and for the axial-torsional tests
available in the literature, respectively.
B5: 2. Beam model for the analysis of metallic wire ropes
B6: Wire ropes are artifacts that, when subjected to suitable loading con-
ditions, undergo geometric rearrangements of the internal structure due
to the relative sliding between individual wires. The formulation of a
continuum model that can properly describe the prevailing mechanical
response should account for this essential feature. In the here proposed
model, this aspect is accounted for by the introduction of eigenstrains
affecting the constitutive response of individual wires, as shown below.
B7: The constructed beam model is based on the shear-deformable beam
theory, also known as the Timoshenko beam (see, for example, [51]),
B8: and the constitutive relations are obtained using the geometric and
material properties of the wires. The basic assumption characterizing
the model is the possibility to experience different kinds of slip modes
between individual wires, here introduced as a kinematic hypothesis
which considers an incompatible term in the wires’ strain measures
affecting the constitutive response. With the aim of capturing the max-
imum and minimum stiffness across the hysteresis cycles, the model
is developed according to the infinitesimal deformation theory and
assuming that the equilibrium equations can be enforced in the initial
configuration as is typical for a linearized regime.

B57: This case, which is referred to in the literature as the ‘stick state’
(see e.g. [52]), shows the classical beam kinematics and it is expected
to describe well the maximum stiffness attainable by the rope, under
the assumptions that no relative inter-wire sliding occurs. However,
it is found that this assumption leads to an overestimation of the
rope’s maximum stiffness which is an order of magnitude greater than
the experimental one (as shown in the next sections and as already
observed in [53]). A possible explanation is that the magnitude of
the deformations under pure bending for which the rope behaves as a
monolithic beam (i.e., perfect stick state) is so small that it becomes
hard to see it experimentally. From a phenomenological standpoint,
it is observed that the wires within the cable always undergo relative
sliding, resulting in a kinematic model that, at the macroscopic level,
is not compatible with that of a standard beam. As the deformations
increase, the rope undergoes a change of reference configuration af-
fecting the wires’ constitutive response. This feature can be effectively
described making use of the concept of eigenstrain, (see, e.g., [54]) that
is widely exploited in studying dislocations, point defects in solids and
other structural changes as well as by Eshelby in developing his well-
known theory of inclusions [55]. In the linearized regime, the total
compatible strain in the beam is given by the sum of the elastic part

B69: Some remarks about Eq. (18) are necessary to put this more clearly
in the context of the ensuing wire rope model. Firstly, in addition to the
well-known coupling between axial and torsional behavior, a coupling
between shearing and bending behavior is also observed in the present
model. Furthermore, in general, 𝑘𝑖𝑗≠𝑘𝑗 𝑖for 𝑖≠𝑗except for the
perfect stick state and thus the stiffness matrix obtained in this way
is always positive definite but not symmetric except for perfect stick
(which happens to be the case of classical beam theory). As a matter of
a fact, the values of the stiffness matrix coefficients 𝑘11, 𝑘22, 𝑘33, 𝑘41, 𝑘52
and 𝑘63 are the same for all states while 𝑘14, 𝑘25, 𝑘36, 𝑘44, 𝑘55 and 𝑘66
attain different values since the eigenstrains affect the way in which the
wire rope curvatures govern the wires’ strains. Another remark is that
the constitutive matrix in Eq. (18) is independent of the coordinate 𝑠
and preserves the given structure only when all wires belong to the
same stick–slip state. Breaking the symmetry in the wires’ stick–slip
states would lead to the emergence of different stiffness matrix coeffi-
cients with values depending on the position along the rope centerline.
Despite this model opens up the possibility for more detailed analyses,
in this work it is only shown how the aforementioned limit hypotheses
lead to sufficiently accurate predictions of the maximum and minimum
stiffnesses in agreement with experimental measurements.
B70: 3. Experimental tests on short wire-ropes subjected to bending
B71: A quasi-static experimental campaign was performed to collect a set
of representative results to be used for the validation of the model. A
single-strand and a multi-strand wire rope, with cross-sections shown
in Fig. 2, were tested by making use of the universal material testing
machine MTS 810 equipped with a custom-made setup, shown in Fig. 3.
The single-strand rope, henceforth denoted by R1, has an average
diameter of 2 cm, while the seven-strands rope, referred to as R2, has
an average diameter of 1 cm.
B72: The target of the experimental tests was the acquisition of the
hysteresis cycles of short wire ropes subjected to pure bending loading
programs. The setup comprised two rigid arms, each one equipped with
two clamps for constraining the ropes’ ends. The clamps were realized
with two steel plates having cylindrical housings to host the ropes
tightened by four bolts. The left arm was rigidly connected to a steel
top plate that, in turn, was anchored to the load cell of the MTS 810.
The right arm was connected to a bottom steel plate fixed to the moving
piston of the testing machine. Fig. 3(a) and (b) show a couple of tested
wire ropes in the reference and deformed configuration, respectively.
In the former, the wire ropes are stress free while, in the latter, they
are subjected to flexural deformation due to upward or downward
movement of the right arm. In order to avoid hardening effects due to
tensile forces in the ropes, a horizontal slider was placed between the
right arm and the bottom steel plate so that the displaced rope ends can
freely approach the left ends. In this configuration, when the right rope
ends are displaced, the load cell connected to the left arm measures the
total restoring force due to the internal shear forces in the ropes.
B73: The force–displacement cycles were acquired in displacement con-
trol by applying a sinusoidal input having a frequency of 0.2 Hz so as
to mimic quasi-static loading conditions. The cycles for different rope
lengths and displacement amplitudes were thus collected: (𝑖) for R1,
lengths equal to 10 cm, 12 cm, 15 cm, 20 cm and amplitude ranging from
2 mm to 50 mm, depending on the rope length; (𝑖𝑖) for R2, lengths equal
to 5 cm, 7.5 cm, 10 cm, 12 cm and amplitude ranging from 2.5 mm to
35 mm, depending on the rope length.

B30: 6. Conclusions
B31: This paper addressed the problem of modeling the mechanical
response of metallic wire ropes aiming at providing robust estimates of
the stiffnesses associated with different regimes, from perfect interwire
stick to full slip.
B32: A new mechanical model employing linear constitutive relation-
ships was presented and proven to accurately predict the upper and
lower bounds of the stiffness which were experimentally measured. The
wire rope is modeled as a classical shear-deformable Timoshenko beam
in the linear regime and the constitutive equations are derived taking
into account accurate wires’ geometry and material properties.
B33: An incompatible eigenstrain term was introduced in the wires’
strain measures to account for the inter-slip between adjacent wires.
The eigenstrains assume different expressions according to the rope’s
strain regime and are assumed to depend linearly on the rope strains.
Specifically, three interwire stick–slip conditions were identified for the
B34: bending regime, namely, the full stick state, the partial stick state, and
the full slip state.
B35: The wire’s constitutive relationships were obtained by extending
Saint-Venant’s theory to curvilinear rods, and the overall rope’s con-
stitutive relations were accordingly obtained. The model is charac-
terized by a generally non-symmetric positive-definite stiffness matrix
exhibiting coupling between bending and shear besides the well-known
coupling between axial and torsional problems.
B36: An experimental campaign was carried out on single- and multi-
strand short wire ropes subjected to pure bending making use of a
tailored experimental apparatus. The experiments were carried out by
subjecting the ropes to quasi-static end displacements by means of a
low frequency input. The results obtained from the experimental tests
in terms of force–displacement cycles at the rope ends were employed
to extract the maximum and minimum stiffness attained across the
hysteresis cycles through a linear regression.
B37: With the present model, the boundary-value problem for the rope
under end displacements could be solved analytically for the cases
examined and the displacements were provided in dimensionless form
together with the explicit expression of the stiffness. The model’s
predictions were compared with the experimental results obtained for
the two ropes tested under bending, showing a good agreement in
both the lower and upper bounds of the stiffness. The theoretical
predictions were also compared with the experimental results available
in the literature concerning single-strand wire ropes subjected to axial-
torsional tests, showing a good agreement. A remarkable feature of this
model is that no calibration of any physical parameter is required to
accurately estimate the magnitude of the limit stiffnesses.
B38: The present linear model provides sufficiently accurate estimates of
the upper/lower stiffnesses. However, the prediction of the hysteresis
cycle or the response under bending and tension still requires further
efforts.
