B0: Nonlinear Vibration Absorber with Pinched Hysteresis:
B1: Theory and Experiments
B2: Biagio Carboni1 and Walter Lacarbonara2
B3: Abstract: A nonlinear vibration absorber exploiting the pinched hysteresis of mixed wire ropes made of NiTiNOL and steel is proposed.
The mixed wire ropes are assembled in a mechanical device which, by bending them, provides the restoring force to the oscillating mass. The
assembly of mixed wire ropes, subject to cyclic end displacements, gives rise to a pinched force-displacement behavior due to the simulta-
neous occurrence of interwire friction and phase transformations. A modified Bouc-Wen model is adopted to represent the pinched hysteresis
while the differential evolutionary (DE) algorithm is employed to identify the constitutive parameters that reproduce the experimental force-
displacement cycles. The DE algorithm is also utilized to optimize the restoring force of the absorber toward mitigation of the dynamic
response of the main structure to external disturbances of various magnitudes. The pinched hysteresis provides an equivalent damping ratio
and resonance frequency, which tend to become almost constant in a given oscillation amplitude range, thus overcoming detrimental detuning
problems typical of other nonlinear absorbers. The absorber performance is evaluated in the context of a multistory steel building model
mounted on a shaking table. The comparison between controlled and uncontrolled responses shows a very good attenuation performance
within the design frequency bandwidth. DOI: 10.1061/(ASCE)EM.1943-7889.0001072. © 2016 American Society of Civil Engineers.

B5: Introduction
B6: Mitigation of the dynamic response of structures subject to excita-
tions is often based on the use of so-called vibration absorbers, also
known as tuned mass dampers (TMDs). A TMD has the role of
applying control forces to the main structure that can introduce pos-
itive damping to reduce the oscillation amplitude. This approach
can be successful in ensuring safety and comfort for structures
in which the architectural requirements are reflected into slender-
ness and, thus flexibility and low damping. Typical civil structures
characterized by high slenderness and low damping are long-span
bridges, footbridges, tall buildings, and skyscrapers. TMDs can be
effective in reducing oscillations caused either by narrow-band
excitations such as wind or by broadband excitations such as
earthquakes.
B7: The TMD concept dates back to 1909, when Frahm [“Device for
damping vibrations of bodies,” U.S. Patent No. 989,958 (1911)]
proposed a vibration control device called dynamic vibration
absorber constituted by a mass connected to the main structure
by a linear spring. The device exploits the phenomenon of anti-
resonance in multi-degrees-of-freedom (MDOF) systems. Later,
in 1928, Den Hartog and Ormondroyd (1928) showed that the
performance of Frahm’s absorber could be improved introducing
damping. They proposed a damped TMD and derived in closed
form the optimal design parameters for the TMD installed on an
B8: undamped single-degree-of-freedom structure (SDOF) under har-
monic excitations (Den Hartog 2013).
B9: The extension of the theory to damped SDOF systems for differ-
ent loading conditions has been investigated by numerous research-
ers. Significant contributions were made by Randall et al. (1981),
Warburton (1982), Warburton and Ayorinde (1980), and Tsai and
Lin (1993). Ioi and Ikeda (1978) proposed empirical design formu-
las relating the tuning frequency and the optimal damping of the
absorber to the mass ratio (i.e., ratio between the TMD mass
and the main mass).
B10: The disadvantages of the linearly damped TMDs led many re-
searchers to investigate nonlinear designs. Inaudi and Kelly (1995)
studied a TMD based on a friction device acting transversely to the
motion. The benefits of this device consist in a lower sensitivity to
temperature changes and an easy adjustment to the desired param-
eters. The main limitation is that the optimization depends on the
excitation level, thus requiring an accurate estimate of the excita-
tion forces. Lim et al. (2006) investigated a hardening Duffing os-
cillator subject to harmonic excitations. Lacarbonara and Vestroni
(2002) proposed the concept of a hysteretic TMD and analyzed its
feasibility in the case of low-rise multistory buildings. Attractive
semiactive TMDs are based on magneto-rheological fluids whose
viscosity can be varied applying a magnetic field to the fluid
(Carlson et al. 1996).
B11: An important class of materials for structural control is repre-
sented by shape memory alloys (SMAs). Williams et al. [“Vibration
absorber using shape memory material,” U.S. Patent No. 6,290,037
(2001)] presented a patent for an adaptive vibration absorber in
which the primary mass is coupled with the secondary mass by
means of a cantilevered beam partly made of SMA. The end mass
is also connected to the primary mass by discrete SMAwires which
may be individually heated through electric current. Dolce et al.
(2000) implemented and tested two families of passive seismic
control SMA devices in the form of braces for new seismic isola-
tion or augmented energy dissipation. Gandhi and Chapuis (2002)
examined the effectiveness of SMA wires for damping out flexural

and demonstrate their potential utilization as adaptive or resilient

B1: Wire ropes are typically employed to resist large loads thanks
to the funicular load-bearing mechanism. In the last century, the
idea of exploiting the complicated mechanical behavior of wire
ropes to dissipate and absorb energy became popular. When a short
wire rope is forced to deform, a relative motion and therefore fric-
tion between individual wires takes place, thereby dissipating en-
ergy. Stockbridge [“Vibration damper,” US Patent No. US1675391
A (1928)] presented a damper based on the interwire friction in
strands with two oscillating masses whose support is fixed onto
power lines to reduce wind-induced vibrations. Wire ropes were
also used in the vibration isolation of devices that control the
passage of fluid to rocket engines (Tinker and Cutchins 1992).
Demetriades et al. (1993) used wire rope springs in seismic protec-
tion of sensitive equipment in buildings. Gerges and Vickery (2003,
2005) studied experimentally the performance of a class of TMDs
which rely on wire rope springs. Carpineto et al. (2014) and
Vestroni et al. patented [“Hysteretic tuned mass damper for
passive control of mechanical vibration,” Italian Patent No.
RM2011A000434 (2011)] a vibration absorber (VA) based on
the hysteretic restoring force of short clamped-clamped steel ropes
subject to flexural cycles. They showed good performance of the
device in reducing the oscillation amplitude of a simply-supported
beam subject to harmonic and transient dynamical loads.
B2: In this work, the proposed vibration absorber is designed to ex-
ploit the pinched hysteretic restoring force provided by an assembly
of mixed NiTiNOL-steel wire ropes subject to flexural cycles. The
absorber is an evolution of the rheological device presented in
Carboni et al. (2015). The device is capable of providing different
kinds of hysteretic behavior changing the arrangements of
NiTiNOL strands/wires and steel strands according to three oper-
ating modes [Carboni et al. 2015; L. Walter, C. Biagio, “Hysteretic
tuned-mass damper device (TMD) for passive control of mechani-
cal vibrations,” European Patent Application No. 12773392.1
(2015); Carboni and Lacarbonara 2016]. The phenomenological
description of the hysteresis loops was achieved introducing a
modified version of the Bouc-Wen (BW) model (Bouc 1967; Wen
1976) and employing the differential evolutionary (DE) algorithm
(Storn and Price 1997) for the model parameters identification.
Here, the multiconfiguration multipurpose device proposed in
Carboni et al. (2015) and Lacarbonara and Carboni [“Hysteretic
tuned-mass damper device (TMD) for passive control of mechanical
B3: vibrations,” European Patent Application No. 12773392.1 (2015)]
is employed in one of its operating modes with an assembly of
mixed wire ropes made of NiTiNOL and stainless steel. This
assembly provides quasi-linear-softening force-displacement cycles
with a pinching at the origin. The pinched behavior is due to the
occurrence of interwire friction and martensitic phase transforma-
tions in the shape memory material. In this paper, the optimization
is performed considering a 2DOF system constituted by the princi-
pal mass to which the absorber mass is attached by means of a quasi-
linear-softening pinched (QSP) restoring force. The optimization
process selects the most suitable QSP hysteresis cycles for mitigat-
ing structural oscillations induced by external disturbances. The
QSP restoring force provides an equivalent damping ratio and
resonance frequency that become nearly constant in a given oscil-
lation amplitude range. These features overcome the detrimental
detuning effects exhibited by other nonlinear hysteretic TMDs.
The performance of the QSP vibration absorber mounted on the
top floor of a multistory steel building scale model is tested using
a shaking table. The uncontrolled response is compared with the
controlled response considering several base excitation levels rang-
ing from soft to hard excitations.
B4: NiTiNOL-Steel Wire Ropes and the Absorber
B5: The idea of combining stainless steel and NiTiNOL wires in the
manufacturing of a new wire rope is due to different reasons.
The first stems from the objective of obtaining a cost-effective wire
rope capable of dissipating a great amount of energy without ex-
hibiting inelastic stains. A second aspect is associated with the fact
that the steel wires placed in the external layers facilitate the manu-
facturing process while improving the stability of the rope in con-
sideration of the strong elasticity of the shape memory material.
Finally, the type of steel and shape memory wires and their relative
positions in the wire rope formation can be designed to give rise to
particular constitutive behaviors under tension and bending.
B6: The proposed TMD features the restoring force provided by an
assembly of short wire ropes subject to flexural cycles. The wire
rope has been designed and manufactured using both NITiNOL
and stainless steel wires. The NiTiNOL behaves as a superelastic
alloy at the ambient temperature around the 20°C. The starting aus-
tenite transformation temperature is about −25°C. The wire rope is
single-stranded with 61 wires arranged in layers of different mate-
rials. All wires have a diameter of 0.63 mm and the rope presents a
global diameter of 5.7 mm. The wire rope was manufactured by
In.tra.Flex (Oggiono, Italy) using the design provided by the au-
thors. The manufacturing process offers challenging aspects due
to the strong shape-recovery capacity. The position of the NiTiNOL
layers is optimized against the bending loads acting on the wire
ropes. Fig. 1 shows the 61-wire rope. Hereafter, the mixed-wire
rope will be denoted by NiTi-ST61.

sented in Carboni et al. (2015) and shown in Fig. 2. It consists of a

(PSG) of the system. The secondary frame is constituted by an ad-
ditional vertical bar (B1), aligned with the vertical midaxis of the
frame and having three passing-through circular rods clamped to it.
These rods, one of which is threaded (S3) while two of them have
smooth surfaces (S1 and S2), pass through also the bars denoted by
B2. The smooth rods S1 and S2 bear the bars B2 while the threaded
rod S3 passes through B2 but its diameter is smaller than the

strongly nonlinear optimization problems since there are no restric-

drawn from a Gaussian distribution in the range of variation. The
vectors are combined with simple algebraic formulas and with the
crossover operation, typical of genetic algorithms, and a new im-

B10: The equivalent damping ratio, associated with experimental

B11: Fig. 5 shows the equivalent damping ratio as function of the
displacement amplitude. It has a rather narrow peak for low ampli-
tudes but after a certain amplitude, it becomes almost constant. This
is due to the phase tranformations of the NiTiNOL wires, which
occur at high amplitudes when the wire ropes are subject to high
bending. In fact, the increase of dissipated energy keeps the ratio
between dissipated energy and elastic energy almost constant. This
behavior is particularly suitable for robust TMDs capable of pre-
serving the attenuation efficiency against a wide range of excitation
amplitudes. One of the main drawbacks of nonlinear TMDs
B12: consists in the high sensitivity of the resonance frequency and
damping with respect to displacement amplitude, which gives rise
to detuning when the external excitation amplitude changes.

B26: For a full characterization of the nonlinear dynamic behavior,
path following of the periodic response upon variation of some
of the forcing parameters is carried out. In particular, this study
examined frequency-response curves for fixed ground acceleration
magnitudes. To this end, a continuation procedure based on the

is based on a sequential application of the Newton-Raphson
method on an augmented system of equations. It is based on an
adjoint orthogonality condition through which the solution at the
next step is searched along the normal to the tangent vector in
the current solution point. A full description of this method can
be found in Lacarbonara (2013).
B27: The main advantage of the implemented path-following method
based on the Poincaré Map lies in the fact that the procedure, by
which the Jacobian of the map and the derivative of the map with
respect to the control parameter are computed via finite differences,
furnishes the monodromy matrix as a by-product of the continu-
ation from which the stability of the periodic solutions can be as-
certained. In the present case, the control parameter coincides with
the nondimensional excitation frequency ~Ω and the Poincaré Map
is evaluated with a fourth-order Runge-Kutta integration scheme
over an interval equal to the excitation period. The algorithm is
implemented in MATLAB.

providing a shear-type behavior to the building model. The steel
L-shaped columns (i.e., without reinforcements) have a low
torsional stiffness, leading to the fact that the frequency of the tor-
sional mode would be very close to the frequency of the lowest
translational mode (i.e., along the wider floor side). For this reason,
the profiles are stiffened against torsional rotations by welding
two triangular plates between the angles of the columns at every
joint with the beams. The steel constituting the L-shaped profiles
and the concrete rebars have characteristic tensile strengths equal
to ftk ¼ 235 and ftk ¼ 450 MPa, respectively. The characteristic
B1: tensile strength of the concrete is σck ¼ 35 MPa. Figs. 15(a–c)
show the front view and the building model from two different
points of view, respectively.
B2: The total mass of the building is 677 kg, of which 203.27 kg is
structural mass due to the columns, beams, plates, and bolts while
the remaining part is the mass of the concrete slabs. The latter do
not have exactly the same mass, which from the first to the last
floor, is equal to 101.45, 99.7, 99.175, 83.7, and 89.75 kg, respec-

mass on the top floor of the building. Thus, their masses must be

B2: Conclusions
B3: A new nonlinear vibration absorber based on the hysterisis of
NiTiNOL and steel wire ropes has been proposed (Carboni et al.
2015). To this end, new single-stranded mixed ropes constituted by
layers of NiTiNOL and steel wires were manufactured. The vibra-
tion absorber exploits the damping due to the interwire friction
and phase transformations together with a nonlinear mechanism
capable of inducing geometric nonlinearities. These features give
rise to a new rheological device that can operate according to four
different constitutive behaviors: quasi-linear-softening hysteresis,
quasi-linear-softening pinched hysteresis (QSP), strongly pinched
hardening hysteresis, and slightly pinched hardening hysteresis.
B4: The pinched hysteresis has been described mathematically em-
ploying a modified version of the Bouc-Wen model. Moreover, the
identification of the material parameters, on the basis of the exper-
imental measurements, has been accurately performed by means of
a variant of the differential evolutionary algorithm.
B5: The hysteretic absorber has been characterized by computing
the frequency-response curves. The quasi-linear-softening pinched
restoring force has been found to exhibit a suitable behavior for
mitigating vibrations in the main structure. For increasing excita-
tion amplitudes, the absorber maintains a nearly constant resonance
frequency. Moreover, thanks to the phase transformations in the
NiTiNOL wires, the ratio between elastic and dissipated energy
is almost constant for a large displacement range. These dynamical
features have been observed experimentally through shaking table
tests of the TMD alone, thus validating the numerical predictions.
B6: The optimization of the TMD material properties for controlling
the resonant dynamics of a linear structure based on the DE
algorithm has shown that the QSP restoring force is the optimal
restoring force for mitigating vibrations caused by different
excitation levels. The consequence is that the typical detuning
phenomenon of other nonlinear hysteretic absorbers, due to the sen-
sitive dependence on the oscillation amplitude, can be overcome.
B7: A scaled multistory steel building has been adopted as case
study for validating experimentally the hysteretic TMD perfor-
mance. The constitutive behavior of the absorber has been
optimized to control the building’s oscillations dominated by its
lowest sway mode. Harmonic tests for different excitation ampli-
tudes in a frequency bandwidth centered about the sway mode have
been performed on the uncontrolled and controlled configurations.
The TMD characterized by the QSP hysteresis has shown very
good attenuation performance, reducing considerably the reso-
nance peaks for different excitation scenarios.
B8: The advantage of the proposed hysteretic absorber over other
devices consists in it capability of providing different hysteresis
shapes according to the considered application. To control a
linear-elastic structure, the traditional linear vibration absorbers be-
have practically as linear systems only within a small displacement
range. On the contrary, the larger displacement range in which both
damping and stiffness are almost constant represents an optimal
range for the proposed nonlinear absorber.
