B13: The cable consists of an assemblage of wires laid helically around a core. The cable is
B14: widely used as an efficient structure for use in cable-stayed structures, for signal
B15: transmission and for power transmission. For overhead power transmission cables, both
B16: strength and dynamic characteristics are critical design parameters. Due to the inherently
B17: low damping characteristics of long span cables, vibration can result in damage to
B18: transmission cables and shorten their useful life. Moreover, for cable-stayed structures,
B19: vibrations can lead to a decrease in the cable load-carrying capacity.
In addition,
B20: agreement on helical cable designs is difficult.
Cable design choices include jacket
B21: materials, water blocking techniques, and the number of fibers to place within the cable.
B22: The cable design chosen depends on the cable's intended application.
Each different
B23: application may require a slightly different cable design. The cable must meet minimal
B24: levels of performance in safety and durability (able to withstand shock, vibration, etc.).
B25: These current design concerns suggest that the dynamic behavior of cables need to be
B26: better understood.
B27: Determination of various mechanical characteristics of helical cables has never been an
B28: easy task. Substantial work has been accomplished over the past twenty years to model
B29: the structural behavior of such cables. The most important dynamical property of cables
B30: is wire slippage that creates large variations in flexural rigidity.
Various flexural
B31: rigidities have been predicted by a number of previously developed models. In addition,

B12: The main focus of recent research has been the response of the cable under dynamic

B19: parts was simulated as a special case of static hysteresis. The slowly varying amplitude
B20: method and the phase method were used in the simulation to identify the damping
B21: parameters ofthe physical cable.
B22: Gatti-Bono and Perkins (2003) conducted an analysis of a two-dimensional towed cable
B23: in an attempt to cover all aspects of cable under tension, torsion, and two-axis bending.
B24: A numerical algorithm based on separate finite differencing in time and in space is
B25: described.
Seabed contact forces are added by modeling the seabed as an elastic
B26: foundation with a prescribed profile. In deriving the motion of the cable, the cable was
B27: treated as a one-dimensional dynamic elastic inextensible continuum.

A simplified
B13: cable vibration model, the frictional bending model (FBM), was developed to estimate

B16: study showed some agreement with experimental data.
B17: All of these investigations address the improvements of dynamic analyses of the cable,
B18: and that the effects of internal dry friction between helical wire layers of a cable playa

Although these research studies
B20: illustrate different applications of the cable, the major focus has been the dynamic
B21: response of these cables to external load and internal frictions.
Their results provide
B22: methods for cable dynamic analyses, and are very helpful for the design of cable systems.
B23: Some of these analyses have resulted in rather complex stiffness matrixes, while others
B24: have focused on individual wires of cable layers, which may not be easily applied to
B25: other types ofcables.

B28: caused by relative wire slippage, which allows a faster identification of the vibration

B13: 1.2. Problem Statement
B14: The flexible cable is complex structure made of multiple layers of helically-served and
B15: axial components with dissimilar materials.
The cable can have a nonlinear structural
B16: characteristic where deformations are large, materials have nonlinear stress-strain curves
B17: and frictional sliding of helical wires occrues. Consequently, the governing equation of
B18: motion of a cable can be a highly nonlinear. Because the dynamic behavior of cable is
B19: not fully understood, especially when the loads applied on the cable are a combination of
B20: tension, torsion and compression, the analytical solution can be impractical to achieve.
B21: Therefore, numerical methods have to be employed.
B22: This study uses the flexural rigidity-curvature relationship predicted by CableCAD®
B23: (2003) into the equation of motion of the cable. In this study, the motion of the cable is
B24: simulated by applying variational theory and using a fifth-order Hermite interpolation
B25: function (Ross, 1998).
The numerical solution of the equation of motion of cable is
B26: achieved by using Newmark's Method (Stein, 2002) with linear acceleration.

B6: 3.2.2
Bending Behavior of Cable Layers
B7: For a cable with small curvature and suffcient applied tension, the shear forces between
B8: layers and wires are relatively small in magnitude and can not overcome the friction
B9: between layers.
Therefore, sliding will not occur, and energy will not be dissipated
B10: during the bending cycle.
In this case, the bending moment-curvature relationship is
B11: linear and the assumption ofplane-sections remaining plane is observed.
B12: If the cable is preformed when manufactured so that large radial pressures are developed
B13: between layers, internal frictional forces can be developed without the application of
B14: tension. By controlling the degree of perform as a design parameter, the desired degree
B15: ofinternal frication can be developed to dissipate energy from the vibrating cable.

B17: 3.3.2
Methods of Direct Integration
B18: There are many methods for advancing the solution ofEq. (3.20) in time. Essentially, the
B19: problem has been reduced to solving a set of coupled ordinary differential equations. For
B20: each time step, the following procedures occur for each element. Given the general nodal
B21: displacements, velocities, and acting forces in time, T, sum the external forces and use
B22: the resultant forces acting on the masses at the nodes to predict accelerations at the nodes.
B23: From the displacements, velocities, and accelerations at time, t, and at a time step, a new
B24: set ofgeneral nodal displacements can be found.
B25: The algorithms commonly used can be categorized into two types, explicit methods and
B26: implicit methods.
Explicit methods, which predict the next state using the element
B27: matrices, do not require a large set of equations to be solved at each time interval. The
B28: equations for one element only calculate forces at neighboring nodes explicitly connected
B29: to a node given a displacement. If in one time step, only these immediate neighbors are

B12: of the solution.
The explicit methods cope well with nonlinearities; eg., plastic
B13: deformation. However, the explicit methods provide unstable solutions if time steps used
B14: are too large.
B15: On the other hand, implicit methods permit a much larger time step, but introduce
B16: artificial damping, and require a pseudo-static problem (including inertia loading) to be
B17: solved at each time interval. In structural dynamic problems implicit integration schemes
B18: give acceptable solutions with time steps usually one or two orders of magnitude larger

B11: 4.4.1
Error Analysis
B12: The solution follows an incremental iterative scheme for physical and numerical reasons.
B13: In this case, there are three main sources of errors to be estimated.
B14: First, the stiffness matrix simulates the cable dynamical propriety, but it might not be the
B15: same as the actual stiffness matrix of a real cable.
For example, the cable's EI curve
B16: shows as many jumps as the number of wire layers that slip.
When the cable passes
B17: through a jump, its dynamic propriety changes rapidly. In addition, the stiffness matrix
B18: in this study focuses on the bending behavior of the cable, and does not cover the
B19: proprieties ofthe cable undergoing the torsion and tension.
B20: Secondly, there are some scratches and dents on the surfaces of cable specimens tested
B21: for interlayer friction (see Appendix A). They are caused during cable manufacture and
B22: result in measurement error.
B23: Thirdly, there might be an accumulation of errors during the equation solving process.
B24: Because the length of each element must not be smaller than the longest half-lay length,
B25: which is a relatively large element length, it may cause errors during the finite element
B26: approach.
Before iteration, least square curve fitting technique is applied and forms
B27: smooth curves for cable configuration.
Table 4.1 shows the sum of the squared error
B28: after the first iteration for the simply supported case. Furthermore, EI of some elements
B29: are fixed, for example, in the simply supported case, flexural rigidities ofthe first and last

B14: In the this study, the cable is considered as a system of helical wires and a core with
B15: distributed dry friction forces at their interfaces and modeled as a one-dimensional
B16: continuum, where the wires are not being modeled individually.
B17: The simplified equation of motion governing the cable vibration is derived, which is a
B18: nonlinear, fourth order partial differential equation with respect to the span of the cable
B19: and the second order derivative with respect to time. The finite element method (FEM) is
B20: applied to solve this equation. The partial differential equation was discretized into time
B21: and space domains.
B22: This model also is used to analyze cable vibration. An implicit expression for predicting
B23: the next state using the elemental matrices is derived. Numerical examples are given to
B24: show the response history.
B25: Since researchers have been studying the cable dynamic behavior, many models have
B26: been developed with different approaches but there was not a unified model that could be
B27: used by all manufacturers and engineers.
The model established in this study can be

B13: 6.2. Future Study
B14: For future studies, an analytical bending model of cable might be extended to include the
B15: effects oftorsion, axial elongation, interlayer pressures, and both ofmaterial nonlinearity
B16: and geometric nonlinearity. A new stiffness matrix may be constructed based on those
B17: effects.
B18: The determination of interlayer pressure would require more accurate experimental work.
B19: Pressure indicating sensor films could be used to obtain a more accurate measurement of
B20: the interlayer pressure.
B21: The effect of internal damping also may be interesting for future studies. Also, in order
B22: to include the external damping effect, the damped vibration of cable should be
B23: formulated.
B24: The current model is based on the small deformation assumption. In order to obtain more
B25: realistic results, the large motion theory should be applied.
