## Proposed Thesis Title

### English

Modeling and Experimental Characterization of Interlayer Slip and Bending Stiffness in Vacuum Layer-Jamming Beams

### Vietnamese

Mô hình hóa và đặc trưng thực nghiệm sự trượt giữa các lớp và độ cứng uốn của dầm kẹt lớp chân không

## Problem Statement

The bending response of vacuum layer-jamming beams is difficult to predict through the transition from partial interaction to gross interlayer slip. Constant-friction or fully bonded models may misestimate stiffness and slip onset, while leakage and envelope viscoelasticity can obscure the underlying interfacial mechanics.

## Research Objective

Develop and experimentally validate a reduced-order mechanics model that relates vacuum pressure, layer count, and bending load to partial-interaction stiffness, interlayer slip onset, and quasi-static hysteresis, while separating interfacial behavior from apparatus and envelope effects.

## Preliminary Research Question

For one vacuum layer-jamming beam architecture under rate-controlled quasi-static bending, how do vacuum pressure, layer count, and bending amplitude govern effective stiffness and the transition from pre-slip to gross interlayer slip, and can an interpretable partial-interaction model predict these responses on held-out test conditions more accurately than fully bonded, fully unbonded, and constant-friction baseline models?

## Scientific Hypothesis

Increasing vacuum pressure raises transferable interlayer shear force and delays gross slip, producing a measurable transition from partially interacting layers toward monolithic bending; a reduced-order model with independently identifiable interface parameters will predict stiffness and slip onset across pressures and layer counts better than limiting-case and constant-friction baselines.

## Why This Direction

- D1 addresses the widely applicable vacuum layer-jamming mechanism and is supported by a broader evidence base than D2.
- It avoids D2's pressure-vessel safety burden and dependence on one duplicated positive-pressure study.
- The partial-interaction and slip-transition problem is a genuine constitutive mechanics question if interface parameters are independently measured and predictions are tested out of sample.
- D2's proposed saturation target may be explained largely by the fully stuck stiffness limit and frame compliance, weakening its scientific contribution.
- The revised D1 can use standard bending, pressure, and edge-displacement measurements without requiring inaccessible internal warping measurements or a prolonged fatigue campaign.

## Expected Contribution

- A falsifiable pressure–slip–stiffness relationship for a controlled vacuum layer-jamming beam
- An experimentally validated reduced-order model with physically interpretable interface parameters
- Quantified validity limits of fully bonded, fully unbonded, and constant-friction approximations
- An experimental protocol that distinguishes interfacial slip from vacuum loss and envelope relaxation
- Design guidance for selecting pressure and layer count to achieve a target stiffness or slip threshold

## MSc Feasibility

Feasible within an MSc if restricted to one beam family, approximately three pressure levels, three layer counts, a few bending amplitudes, and three specimen replicates. The main experiments require a UTM or instrumented bending rig, regulated vacuum, pressure logging, and accessible optical tracking. Short conditioning sequences are sufficient; long-term wear and fatigue claims must not be included. A staged design should first verify repeatable slip and rule out leakage before expanding the test matrix.

## Publication Path

A WoS/Scopus journal paper is plausible if the study demonstrates reproducible slip transitions, independently identifiable interface parameters, held-out predictive validation, uncertainty analysis, and a meaningful improvement over classical limiting-case and constant-friction models. A purely fitted pressure–stiffness curve or single-specimen characterization would likely be insufficient.

## Unresolved Novelty Risks

- Classical partial-interaction, shear-lag, or multi-leaf-spring theory may already explain much of the response after straightforward adaptation.
- Published layer-jamming models may already include Coulomb slip, pressure-dependent friction, or equivalent transition criteria.
- The measurable response may be dominated by envelope mechanics rather than interlayer slip.
- A simple limiting-case interpolation may predict the data as accurately as the proposed reduced-order formulation.
- The contribution may remain specific to one material pair unless its dimensionless or mechanistic transferability is demonstrated.

## Required Searches After Submission

- Scopus/Web of Science: ('layer jamming' OR 'laminar jamming') AND ('partial interaction' OR 'shear lag' OR 'interlayer slip' OR 'beam model').
- Forward and backward citation searches from Narang, Kim, Kawamura, and the four D1 supporting papers.
- Classical Newmark, Goodman, multi-leaf-spring, frictionally laminated beam, and partial-composite-action literature.
- Models of pressure-dependent contact, pre-sliding friction, microslip, and hysteresis in layered beams.
- Experimental methods for measuring interlayer slip in vacuum-jammed laminates.
- Studies separating envelope viscoelasticity, Mullins effects, vacuum leakage, and interface friction in soft pneumatic or jamming structures.
- Patents and non-journal prior art on vacuum layer-jamming beam mechanics and stiffness prediction.

## Submission Rationale

Revised D1 offers the strongest balance of mechanical rigor, falsifiability, laboratory accessibility, and publication potential. Its defensible core is not cyclic wear or hidden warping, but the pressure-controlled transition between partial interaction and gross interlayer slip. The provisional title describes that investigation without claiming novelty or committing prematurely to a specific friction law. Selection remains conditional on an early literature search and pilot tests confirming that slip is measurable and not masked by leakage or envelope relaxation.
